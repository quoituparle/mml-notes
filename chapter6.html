Chapter 6 · Probability and Distributions

A language for uncertainty: noisy data, unknown parameters, and honest predictions.

Why this chapter?

Real data is noisy, and a model is never 100% sure. Probability gives us a precise way to say how unsure. In ML you'll use it three ways: to model noise in data (a likelihood), to express beliefs about parameters (a prior), and to update beliefs after seeing data (Bayes' theorem).

6.1Construction of a Probability Space

Probability theory starts from three ingredients (the book's "probability space"):

  • The sample space $\Omega$: all possible outcomes of an experiment. Two coin tosses: $\{hh,ht,th,tt\}$.
  • The event space $\mathcal{A}$: the sets of outcomes we can ask about (e.g. "at least one head").
  • The probability $P$: a number in $[0,1]$ for each event, with $P(\Omega)=1$.

In practice we rarely work with $\Omega$ directly. We use a random variable $X:\Omega\to\mathcal{T}$, which turns each outcome into a quantity we care about. (The book's joke: it is "neither random nor a variable. It is a function.") Counting heads in two tosses: $X(hh)=2$, $X(ht)=X(th)=1$, $X(tt)=0$.

ExampleBook Example 6.1: drawing two coins

A bag gives a $ coin with probability $0.3$ (draws are independent). Let $X$ = number of $ coins in two draws.

$P(X=2)=0.3\cdot0.3=0.09,\quad P(X=1)=2\cdot0.3\cdot0.7=0.42,\quad P(X=0)=0.7\cdot0.7=0.49$.

$X=1$ gets two outcomes ($\$\pounds$ and $\pounds\$$), so it gets the sum of two probabilities. The widget in 6.2 reproduces this.

Going deeper: why probability is "the" logic of uncertainty

Plausible reasoning. Classical logic only knows true/false. Real reasoning is graded: your friend is late; you can't prove traffic did it, but traffic ($H_2$) becomes more plausible than "on time" ($H_1$, ruled out) and far more plausible than "abducted by aliens" ($H_3$). The Cox–Jaynes theorem says: if plausibilities are real numbers, follow common sense, and are consistent (the same evidence always gives the same value; all available data is used; equal knowledge gives equal plausibility), then they must obey the rules of probability. So probability is not an arbitrary choice, it is the unique consistent extension of logic.

Two interpretations. Bayesian: probability is a degree of belief (subjective, can be assigned to one-off events and to parameters). Frequentist: probability is the long-run relative frequency of an event. Machine learning uses both views; later chapters (maximum likelihood vs Bayesian regression) will show each in action.

The three ingredients, precisely

  • Sample space $\Omega$: all outcomes (two coin tosses: $\{hh,ht,th,tt\}$).
  • Event space $\mathcal A$: the collection of subsets we can assign probabilities to. For discrete $\Omega$ it is simply all subsets (the power set). For continuous $\Omega$ you must restrict to well-behaved sets (a Borel $\sigma$-algebra), because "all subsets" is too wild. We won't need the technicalities.
  • Probability $P$: $P(A)\in[0,1]$, $P(\Omega)=1$, and for disjoint events $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)$.

Random variable as a pre-image. For $X:\Omega\to\mathcal T$ and $S\subseteq\mathcal T$: $P_X(S)=P(X\in S)=P\big(X^{-1}(S)\big)=P(\{\omega:X(\omega)\in S\})$. In Ex. 6.1, $S=\{1\}$ has pre-image $\{(\$,\pounds),(\pounds,\$)\}$, which is why $P(X=1)=0.3\cdot0.7+0.7\cdot0.3=0.42$. The distribution $P_X$ (also "law") is the probability each value of $\mathcal T$ gets.

Practical tip. First decide whether your quantity is categorical (discrete: class labels, words) or continuous (heights, prices). That tells you which tools (mass functions or densities) to reach for.

Probability vs statistics (book): probability starts from a model and works out what data to expect. Statistics starts from observed data and works out the model. Machine learning lives mostly on the statistics side, using probability's rules.

6.2Discrete and Continuous Probabilities

Discrete (countable values)probability mass function $P(X=x)$add up the masses
Continuous (real numbers)probability density function $p(x)$cumulative distribution function $P(X\le x)=\int_{-\infty}^xp(t)\,dt$

Discrete: the probability mass function

Each value has its own probability, and they add to 1. The Binomial distribution counts successes in $N$ independent yes/no trials with success chance $\mu$ (book Example 6.9):

$$p(m\mid N,\mu)=\binom Nm\mu^m(1-\mu)^{N-m},\qquad \mathbb{E}[m]=N\mu,\quad \mathbb{V}[m]=N\mu(1-\mu)$$

Continuous: the density

For a continuous variable the probability of exactly one value is 0. Instead, the density $p(x)$ gives probability as area: $P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bp(x)\,dx$, and $\int p(x)dx=1$.

Common trap (from the book): a density can be bigger than 1. A very narrow bump must be tall to have area 1. Only areas are probabilities. You'll see this in the Gaussian widget in 6.5.

Going deeper: joint, marginal, conditional, pdf and cdf

A discrete bivariate distribution from counts

If $n_{ij}$ events had $X=x_i$ and $Y=y_j$ out of $N$ total (book Fig. 6.2), then

$$P(X=x_i,Y=y_j)=\frac{n_{ij}}N\ \text{(joint)},\quad P(X=x_i)=\frac{c_i}N=\frac{\sum_jn_{ij}}N\ \text{(marginal)},\quad P(Y=y_j\mid X=x_i)=\frac{n_{ij}}{c_i}\ \text{(conditional)}.$$

$c_i$ is the column sum, $r_j$ the row sum. Marginals add up row/column totals; conditionals are "a cell as a fraction of its column (or row)". Everything sums to 1. (Exercise E6.1 practises exactly this.) Notation warning: texts write $p(x)$ for the mass function, the density, and even the CDF; read the context.

Continuous: precise definitions

  • A pdf is any $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ with $f(\mathbf x)\ge0$ and $\int f=1$; it defines a random variable via $P(a\le X\le b)=\int_a^bf(x)dx$.
  • $P(X=x)=0$ for every single $x$ (an interval of width zero has no area). That's not a contradiction: individual points have probability 0, intervals don't.
  • The cdf of a multivariate $X$: $F_X(\mathbf x)=P(X_1\le x_1,\dots,X_D\le x_D)=\int_{-\infty}^{x_1}\!\!\cdots\!\int_{-\infty}^{x_D}f$. It is non-decreasing, runs from 0 to 1, and (where differentiable) $f=F'$.

Book Ex. 6.3 Discrete versus continuous uniform

A discrete uniform variable on 3 states $\{-1.1,\,0.3,\,1.5\}$ gives each state mass $\tfrac13$ (the states' numerical values and order are irrelevant). A continuous uniform on $[0.9,1.6]$ has constant density $\frac1{1.6-0.9}\approx1.43$, greater than 1, yet the area $1.43\times0.7=1$. Densities can exceed 1; masses cannot.

Named discrete example (Bernoulli, book Ex. 6.8): a yes/no experiment with success probability $\mu$: $p(x\mid\mu)=\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}$, $x\in\{0,1\}$, mean $\mu$, variance $\mu(1-\mu)$. The Binomial counts successes in $N$ Bernoulli trials.

In short. Discrete: heights are probabilities. Continuous: areas are probabilities. The CDF accumulates them.

6.3Sum Rule, Product Rule, and Bayes' Theorem

Two variables $x,y$ have a joint distribution $p(x,y)$. There are only two rules:

$$\underbrace{p(x)=\sum_yp(x,y)\ \ \Big(\text{or }\int p(x,y)\,dy\Big)}_{\text{sum rule (marginalisation)}}\qquad\qquad\underbrace{p(x,y)=p(y\mid x)\,p(x)}_{\text{product rule}}$$

The sum rule "sums out" the variable you don't care about. The product rule factors the joint into "marginal × conditional". Try both on this table (it's a joint distribution of two variables with 3 states each):

Bayes' theorem

The product rule can be written two ways, $p(x,y)=p(y\mid x)p(x)=p(x\mid y)p(y)$. Equate them:

$$\underbrace{p(x\mid y)}_{\text{posterior}}=\frac{\overbrace{p(y\mid x)}^{\text{likelihood}}\ \overbrace{p(x)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{p(y)}_{\text{evidence}}},\qquad p(y)=\int p(y\mid x)p(x)\,dx$$
  • Prior $p(x)$: what you believed about the unknown $x$ before seeing data.
  • Likelihood $p(y\mid x)$: how likely the observed data $y$ would be if $x$ were true.
  • Posterior $p(x\mid y)$: what you believe after. This is what you want.
  • Evidence $p(y)$: just the normaliser that makes the posterior sum to 1.

Humans are famously bad at this. The classic illustration (a standard example, not from the book): a disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of sick people but also gives a false alarm for 9% of healthy ones. You test positive. How worried should you be? Most people say "~90%". Look at the dots:

Because the disease is rare, the healthy people who falsely test positive (orange) outnumber the sick people who correctly do (blue). So a positive result means only about 9% chance of being sick. Raise the prior and watch the posterior jump. This is the whole reason priors matter.

ML link. Every Bayesian model is this theorem with: $x$ = parameters (or a class label), $y$ = data. Chapter 8–9 build on it.

Going deeper: the fine print of Bayes, and a worked example

  • Sum rule for many variables: $p(x_i)=\int p(x_1,\dots,x_D)\,d\mathbf x_{\setminus i}$ (integrate out all others). Computing such high-dimensional sums/integrals is the main computational difficulty of probabilistic modelling: no known polynomial-time exact algorithm in general.
  • Product rule chained: $p(x_1,\dots,x_n)=p(x_1)\,p(x_2\mid x_1)\cdots p(x_n\mid x_1,\dots,x_{n-1})$ (repeat the rule). This factorisation underlies sequence models and graphical models (Chapter 8).
  • Prior: beliefs about $x$ before data. Any prior is allowed, but it must be nonzero for every plausible $x$: if $p(x)=0$ no amount of data can ever revive $x$.
  • Likelihood $p(y\mid x)$: a distribution over data $y$ for fixed $x$, not a distribution over $x$ (as a function of $x$ it needn't sum to 1). Say "the likelihood of $x$", never "the likelihood of $y$".
  • Evidence $p(y)=\int p(y\mid x)p(x)dx=\mathbb{E}_X[p(y\mid x)]$: the expected likelihood under the prior. It only normalises the posterior, but it is also the key quantity for comparing models (Chapter 8).
  • Keep the whole posterior if you can. Summaries such as its mode (MAP) discard information; decision-making and active learning often need the full posterior.

Worked example: which coin?

A drawer holds a fair coin ($P(\text{heads})=0.5$) and a biased one ($0.8$). You pick one at random ($p=\tfrac12$ each), flip it, and see heads. Evidence: $p(H)=0.5\cdot0.5+0.8\cdot0.5=0.65$. Posterior: $P(\text{biased}\mid H)=\dfrac{0.8\cdot0.5}{0.65}=\dfrac{0.4}{0.65}\approx0.615$. One flip moved your belief in "biased" from $50\%$ to $61.5\%$. (More flips keep updating it; the Beta–Bernoulli widget in 6.6 does exactly that for a coin with unknown bias.)

In short. Sum rule: marginalise. Product rule: joint = conditional × marginal. Bayes: posterior ∝ likelihood × prior.

6.4Summary Statistics and Independence

Mean, variance, covariance

The expected value is the probability-weighted average:

$$\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\sum_xg(x)P(x)\quad\text{or}\quad\int g(x)p(x)\,dx,\qquad \text{mean }\ \mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$$
QuantityFormulaMeaning
Variance$\mathbb{V}[X]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2$Spread around the mean ("mean of the square minus square of the mean")
Covariance$\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu_X)(Y-\mu_Y)]$Do they move together (+) or oppositely (−)?
Correlation$\operatorname{corr}[X,Y]=\dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]}{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[X]\mathbb{V}[Y]}}\in[-1,1]$Covariance with the units removed

For a vector $\mathbf{x}$ the covariance is a matrix $\Sigma$ (symmetric positive semi-definite, as in Ch. 3–4). Its diagonal holds the variances. Other "typical value" statistics: the median (middle value), the mode (most likely value).

From data you estimate these empirical versions, which are just averages:

$$\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N\mathbf{x}_n,\qquad \Sigma=\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N(\mathbf{x}_n-\bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_n-\bar{\mathbf{x}})^\top$$

Book remark: the raw-score formula $\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2$ is handy in derivations, but subtracting two huge, nearly equal numbers can be numerically unstable on a computer.

Sums and linear transformations

$$\mathbb{E}[\mathbf x+\mathbf y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf x]+\mathbb{E}[\mathbf y],\qquad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf x+\mathbf y]=\mathbb{V}[\mathbf x]+\mathbb{V}[\mathbf y]+\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]+\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf y,\mathbf x]$$
$$\mathbf y=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\mathbb{E}[\mathbf y]=A\boldsymbol\mu+\mathbf b,\qquad \mathbb{V}[\mathbf y]=A\Sigma A^\top$$

The second line is the same sandwich shape as the change of basis in Chapter 2.

Independence

$X,Y$ are independent if knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

$$p(x,y)=p(x)\,p(y)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ p(y\mid x)=p(y)$$

Independent ⇒ uncorrelated (covariance 0), but not the other way round in general. For jointly Gaussian variables, though, uncorrelated does mean independent.

Fun fact from the book (6.4.6): covariance is an inner product for random variables, and correlation is the cosine of the angle between them. All of Chapter 3's geometry applies.

Going deeper: mean, median, mode, empirical statistics, and independence

Expected value of a function; three "averages"

For a function $g$ of $X\sim p$: $\mathbb{E}[g(X)]=\int g(x)p(x)dx$ (or $\sum_xg(x)P(x)$). Linearity: $\mathbb{E}[ag(X)+bh(X)]=a\mathbb{E}[g(X)]+b\mathbb{E}[h(X)]$. For vectors, take it elementwise. The mean is the case $g(x)=x$; the median is the value where the CDF is $0.5$ (robust to outliers; no natural definition in 2D); the mode is a peak of the density (may be several).

Book Ex. 6.4 A bimodal mixture: mean $\ne$ median $\ne$ mode

$p(\mathbf x)=0.4\,\mathcal N\big(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}10\\2\end{smallmatrix}\right],I\big)+0.6\,\mathcal N\big(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\0\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}8.4&2\\2&1.7\end{smallmatrix}\right]\big)$. Marginal in the first coordinate: mean $4$, median $\approx2.80$, a mode near $10$ (and a lower local peak at $0$). Second coordinate: mean $0.8$, median $\approx0.87$, a single mode $\approx1.33$. The 2D mean is $(4,0.8)$ but the 2D modes are near $(10,2)$ and $(0,0)$: the mean can lie where there is almost no probability mass.

Covariance, precisely

  • $\operatorname{Cov}[x,y]=\mathbb{E}[(x-\mathbb{E}x)(y-\mathbb{E}y)]=\mathbb{E}[xy]-\mathbb{E}[x]\mathbb{E}[y]$. Multivariate: $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]=\mathbb{E}[\mathbf x\mathbf y^\top]-\mathbb{E}[\mathbf x]\mathbb{E}[\mathbf y]^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times E}$ (cross-covariance), with $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf y,\mathbf x]=\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]^\top$.
  • The covariance matrix $\mathbb{V}[\mathbf x]=\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf x]=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top]$ is symmetric positive semi-definite; the diagonal holds the variances (marginal spreads), off-diagonals the cross-covariances.
  • Correlation divides by the standard deviations so it is unit-free in $[-1,1]$; the correlation matrix is the covariance matrix of standardised variables. Two datasets can share all means and variances yet differ in covariance (positively vs negatively tilted clouds, book Fig. 6.5).

Empirical (sample) statistics

The formulas above are population quantities. From data $\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_N$ you compute $\bar{\mathbf x}=\frac1N\sum\mathbf x_n$ and $\Sigma=\frac1N\sum(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})(\mathbf x_n-\bar{\mathbf x})^\top$ (symmetric positive semi-definite). The book uses the factor $\frac1N$ (a biased estimate); the unbiased version divides by $N-1$.

Worked example

Three points $(1,2),(2,1),(3,3)$. Mean $(2,2)$. Deviations $(-1,0),(0,-1),(1,1)$. $\Sigma=\tfrac13\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2/3&1/3\\1/3&2/3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and the correlation is $\tfrac{1/3}{2/3}=0.5$. (The widget's empirical readout computes this for 320 samples.)

Three expressions for the variance

(i) the definition $\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]$; (ii) the raw-score form $\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mathbb{E}[X]^2$ ("mean of the square minus square of the mean": one pass over data, but numerically risky when the two terms are huge and close); (iii) the pairwise form $\frac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j}(x_i-x_j)^2=2\big(\frac1N\sum x_i^2-(\frac1N\sum x_i)^2\big)$: the average squared distance between all pairs is twice the variance. Exercises E6.6 and E6.7 prove (ii) and (iii).

Independence, and what it doesn't mean

If $X,Y$ are independent: $p(y\mid x)=p(y)$, $p(x\mid y)=p(x)$, $\mathbb{V}[x+y]=\mathbb{V}[x]+\mathbb{V}[y]$, $\operatorname{Cov}[x,y]=0$. The converse fails because covariance only sees linear dependence.

Book Ex. 6.5 Uncorrelated but dependent

Let $\mathbb{E}[X]=0$ and $\mathbb{E}[X^3]=0$ (for instance $X$ symmetric around 0), and $Y=X^2$. $Y$ is a function of $X$, so completely dependent. But $\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]=\mathbb{E}[X^3]-\mathbb{E}[X]\mathbb{E}[X^2]=0$.

i.i.d. ("independent and identically distributed") data points are the standard assumption in ML: each draw independent of the others and from the same distribution. Conditional independence $X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y\mid Z$ means $p(x,y\mid z)=p(x\mid z)p(y\mid z)$ for every $z$, equivalently $p(x\mid y,z)=p(x\mid z)$: once $z$ is known, $y$ tells you nothing more about $x$.

Random variables as vectors

For zero-mean $X,Y$, $\langle X,Y\rangle:=\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]$ is an inner product (symmetric, bilinear, positive definite up to constants). Then $\|X\|=\sqrt{\mathbb{V}[X]}=\sigma$ (length = standard deviation; length 0 means deterministic), and $\cos\theta=\dfrac{\operatorname{Cov}[X,Y]}{\sigma_X\sigma_Y}$ is the correlation. Uncorrelated means orthogonal, and $\mathbb{V}[X+Y]=\mathbb{V}[X]+\mathbb{V}[Y]$ is literally Pythagoras. (Caution from the book: Euclidean distance between distributions is not the right tool; use divergences such as KL, from information geometry.)

In short. Mean = weighted average. Variance = spread. Covariance matrix $\Sigma$ = all pairwise co-movements. Linear map: $\mu\to A\mu+b$, $\Sigma\to A\Sigma A^\top$.

6.5Gaussian Distribution

The Gaussian (normal) distribution is the bell curve. It is defined by just a mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$:

$$p(x\mid\mu,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)$$

Why is the Gaussian everywhere?

Partly because of the central limit theorem (mentioned in the book): add up many independent random things and the total looks Gaussian, no matter what the individual pieces look like. Check it:

And partly because it's very well-behaved, as the next facts show.

Multivariate Gaussian

In $D$ dimensions it has a mean vector and a covariance matrix:

$$p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|\Sigma|^{-1/2}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big)$$

The contours are ellipses whose shape is given by $\Sigma$ (its eigenvectors, from Ch. 4, are the ellipse axes). Move $\rho$ to tilt the cloud. The vertical line shows conditioning: fix $x_1$ and look at what's left for $x_2$.

ThmMarginals and conditionals of Gaussians are Gaussians

Split a joint Gaussian into parts $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$ with $\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf x\\\mathbf y\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal N\Big(\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\mu_x\\\boldsymbol\mu_y\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_{xx}&\Sigma_{xy}\\\Sigma_{yx}&\Sigma_{yy}\end{bmatrix}\Big)$. Then

$$p(\mathbf x)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_x,\Sigma_{xx})\quad\text{(just drop }\mathbf y\text{)}$$
$$p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_{x|y},\Sigma_{x|y}),\quad \boldsymbol\mu_{x|y}=\boldsymbol\mu_x+\Sigma_{xy}\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}(\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\mu_y),\quad \Sigma_{x|y}=\Sigma_{xx}-\Sigma_{xy}\Sigma_{yy}^{-1}\Sigma_{yx}$$

In the widget: the conditional mean is a straight line in the observed value (green line), and the conditional variance $\sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)$ is smaller than before. Observing one variable always reduces uncertainty about a correlated one. Kalman filters and Gaussian processes are built on this formula.

More closure properties

  • Product of two Gaussian densities is (up to a constant) a Gaussian. Precisions add: $C=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}$.
  • Sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian: means add, covariances add.
  • Linear transformation: $\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)\Rightarrow A\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(A\boldsymbol\mu,A\Sigma A^\top)$.
ML link: sampling. To draw samples from $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$: sample $\mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and output $\boldsymbol\mu+L\mathbf z$, where $\Sigma=LL^\top$ is the Cholesky factorisation (Ch. 4). That is exactly how the dots in the widget above are generated.

Going deeper: worked Gaussian calculations, mixtures, and sampling

Book Ex. 6.6 Marginalising and conditioning a bivariate Gaussian

$\left[\begin{smallmatrix}x_1\\x_2\end{smallmatrix}\right]\sim\mathcal N\big(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\2\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0.3&-1\\-1&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]\big)$. Marginal (just read off the block): $p(x_1)=\mathcal N(0,\,0.3)$. Conditional on $x_2=-1$: $\mu_{x_1|x_2}=0+(-1)\cdot\tfrac15\cdot(-1-2)=0.6$ and $\sigma^2_{x_1|x_2}=0.3-(-1)\cdot\tfrac15\cdot(-1)=0.1$. So $p(x_1\mid x_2=-1)=\mathcal N(0.6,\,0.1)$: shifted toward the observation (negative covariance: $x_2$ below its mean pushes $x_1$ up) and much narrower than the marginal ($0.1\lt0.3$).

Product of Gaussian densities (the engine of Bayesian regression)

$\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf a,A)\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf b,B)=c\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf c,C)$ with $C=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}$, $\mathbf c=C(A^{-1}\mathbf a+B^{-1}\mathbf b)$ and the scale $c=\mathcal N(\mathbf a\mid\mathbf b,A+B)=\mathcal N(\mathbf b\mid\mathbf a,A+B)$. Precisions add and the new mean is a precision-weighted average. Numeric check in 1D ($a=0,A=1,b=2,B=3$): $C=0.75$, $c=0.5$, $c=\mathcal N(0\mid2,4)\approx0.121$. At $x=1$ the left side is $\mathcal N(1|0,1)\mathcal N(1|2,3)=0.0472$ and the right side $0.121\cdot\mathcal N(1|0.5,0.75)=0.0472$ ✓.

Sums and linear maps

Independent Gaussians: $p(\mathbf x+\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_x+\boldsymbol\mu_y,\Sigma_x+\Sigma_y)$, and $p(a\mathbf x+b\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(a\boldsymbol\mu_x+b\boldsymbol\mu_y,\ a^2\Sigma_x+b^2\Sigma_y)$ (book Ex. 6.7). Linear map: $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x\Rightarrow p(\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(A\boldsymbol\mu,A\Sigma A^\top)$. Reverse direction: if $\mathbf y\sim\mathcal N(A\mathbf x,\Sigma)$ with $A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ of full column rank ($M\ge N$), invert the relation with the pseudo-inverse, $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x\iff\mathbf x=(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf y$. Then $\mathbf x$ is a linear map of $\mathbf y$, so (book eq. 6.91)

$$p(\mathbf x)=\mathcal N\Big(\mathbf x\ \Big|\ (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf y,\ \ (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\Sigma A(A^\top A)^{-1}\Big).$$

Mixtures: weighted sum of densities is not a weighted sum of variables

For $p(x)=\alpha p_1(x)+(1-\alpha)p_2(x)$ (Theorem 6.12): $\mathbb{E}[x]=\alpha\mu_1+(1-\alpha)\mu_2$ and

$$\mathbb{V}[x]=\big(\alpha\sigma_1^2+(1-\alpha)\sigma_2^2\big)+\big(\alpha\mu_1^2+(1-\alpha)\mu_2^2-[\alpha\mu_1+(1-\alpha)\mu_2]^2\big).$$

Proof idea: compute $\mathbb{E}[x^2]=\alpha(\mu_1^2+\sigma_1^2)+(1-\alpha)(\mu_2^2+\sigma_2^2)$ and subtract the squared mean. The two brackets are "average within-component variance" plus "variance of the component means", an instance of the law of total variance $\mathbb{V}[x]=\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbb{V}[x\mid y]]+\mathbb{V}_Y[\mathbb{E}[x\mid y]]$. Check with the book's mixture $0.4\,\mathcal N(10,1)+0.6\,\mathcal N(0,8.4)$: $(0.4+5.04)+(40+0-16)=29.44$ ✓. Mixtures of Gaussians return in Chapter 11 (density estimation).

Sampling, in two stages

(1) Computers supply uniform numbers on $[0,1]$; a transformation such as Box–Müller turns pairs of them into standard normal samples (the "Y = Φ⁻¹(U)" idea of 6.7). (2) To get $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\Sigma)$ transform a standard normal vector: $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\mu$ with $AA^\top=\Sigma$; the Cholesky factor (Chapter 4) is the convenient triangular choice.

In short. Gaussian = mean + covariance. Marginals, conditionals, sums, and linear maps of Gaussians stay Gaussian, with closed-form parameters.
Check yourself$X\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. What is the distribution of $2X+3$?
Answer: $\mathcal N(3,\,4)$: mean $A\mu+b=3$, variance $A\Sigma A^\top=4$.

6.6Conjugacy and the Exponential Family

Bayes' theorem needs us to multiply a prior by a likelihood and normalise. Usually that integral is nasty. A conjugate prior is a prior chosen so the posterior is of the same family. Then updating is just changing a couple of numbers.

The classic: Beta prior, coin-flip likelihood

The probability $\mu$ that a coin lands heads lives in $[0,1]$. A Beta distribution is a natural belief over such a number:

$$p(\mu\mid\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1},\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mu]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$$

($\alpha=\beta=1$ is uniform, $\alpha,\beta>1$ is a single hump, $\alpha,\beta<1$ is U-shaped.) Observe $h$ heads and $t$ tails. Multiply prior × likelihood: $\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}\cdot\mu^h(1-\mu)^t$, which is still a Beta:

$$\text{posterior}=\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+h,\ \beta+t)$$

This is book Examples 6.11–6.12. The book's Table 6.2 lists other conjugate pairs:

LikelihoodConjugate prior

Gaussian (unknown variance)Inverse Gamma / Inverse Wishart

Sufficient statistics and the exponential family

A sufficient statistic is a summary of the data that holds all the information about the parameter: for coin flips you only need the counts $h,t$, not the order of the flips. The exponential family is the class of distributions with the form

$$p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=h(\mathbf x)\exp\big(\langle\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\rangle-A(\boldsymbol\theta)\big)$$

where $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)$ are the sufficient statistics. Gaussian, Bernoulli, Binomial, Beta, and many others belong to it (book Examples 6.13–6.14). Why care: exponential-family likelihoods always have a conjugate prior, and they have only finitely many sufficient statistics, which makes learning tractable.

Going deeper: Beta, the Gamma function, sufficient statistics, and the exponential family in detail

Beta distribution facts

$\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)$ lives on $[0,1]$ with $\mathbb{E}[\mu]=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}$ and $\mathbb{V}[\mu]=\frac{\alpha\beta}{(\alpha+\beta)^2(\alpha+\beta+1)}$. The normaliser uses the Gamma function $\Gamma(t)=\int_0^\infty x^{t-1}e^{-x}dx$, with $\Gamma(t+1)=t\,\Gamma(t)$ (so $\Gamma(n)=(n-1)!$ for integers). Intuition: $\alpha$ pulls mass toward 1, $\beta$ toward 0. Special cases: $\alpha=\beta=1$ uniform; $\alpha,\beta\lt1$ U-shaped (bimodal at 0 and 1); $\alpha,\beta\gt1$ unimodal; $\alpha=\beta\gt1$ symmetric around $\tfrac12$.

Conjugacy, derived

Binomial likelihood times Beta prior: $\mu^h(1-\mu)^{N-h}\cdot\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}=\mu^{h+\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{N-h+\beta-1}\propto\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+h,\beta+N-h)$ (book Ex. 6.11). For a single Bernoulli observation $x\in\{0,1\}$ the update is $(\alpha,\beta)\to(\alpha+x,\ \beta+1-x)$ (Ex. 6.12). The Gaussian is conjugate to itself for its mean; the inverse Gamma / inverse Wishart are conjugate for its variance / covariance; the Dirichlet for the multinomial.

Sufficient statistics (Fisher–Neyman)

A statistic is a deterministic function of the data. $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)$ is sufficient for $\theta$ if it carries all the information the data holds about $\theta$. Formally: $p(\mathbf x\mid\theta)=h(\mathbf x)\,g_\theta(\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x))$, where $h$ doesn't involve $\theta$ and $\theta$ enters only through $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)$. Examples: for coin flips, the count of heads (not their order); for a Gaussian sample, the sample mean and mean-square. Pitman–Koopman–Darmois (1930s): exponential families are the only families whose sufficient statistics stay finite-dimensional as the number of i.i.d. samples grows.

The exponential family

$$p(\mathbf x\mid\boldsymbol\theta)=h(\mathbf x)\exp\big(\langle\boldsymbol\theta,\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\rangle-A(\boldsymbol\theta)\big)\ \ \propto\ \exp\big(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)\big)$$

$\boldsymbol\phi$: sufficient statistics; $\boldsymbol\theta$: natural parameters; $A(\boldsymbol\theta)$: the log-partition function (the normaliser, so the density integrates to 1); $h$ can be absorbed by adding $\log h(\mathbf x)$ to $\boldsymbol\phi$.

Book Ex. 6.13 The Gaussian

$\boldsymbol\phi(x)=(x,x^2)$ and $\boldsymbol\theta=\big(\frac{\mu}{\sigma^2},-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\big)$ give $\exp(\theta_1x+\theta_2x^2)\propto\exp\big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\big)$ (complete the square).

Book Ex. 6.14 The Bernoulli, and where the sigmoid comes from

$\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}=\exp\big[x\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\log(1-\mu)\big]$, so $h=1$, $\phi(x)=x$, natural parameter $\theta=\log\frac{\mu}{1-\mu}$ (the "log-odds"), $A(\theta)=\log(1+e^\theta)$. Inverting: $\mu=\frac1{1+e^{-\theta}}$, the sigmoid. So $\theta\in\mathbb{R}$ is squashed into $\mu\in(0,1)$. That is exactly why the sigmoid appears in logistic regression.

Conjugate priors for free: every exponential-family likelihood has a conjugate prior of the form $p(\boldsymbol\theta\mid\boldsymbol\gamma)=h_c(\boldsymbol\theta)\exp\big(\langle\boldsymbol\gamma,[\boldsymbol\theta;\,-A(\boldsymbol\theta)]\rangle-A_c(\boldsymbol\gamma)\big)$. Applying this to the Bernoulli reproduces the Beta distribution (Ex. 6.15). Other benefits: maximum-likelihood estimation reduces to matching empirical sufficient statistics (e.g. sample mean and covariance of a Gaussian), and the log-likelihood is concave, so optimisation (Chapter 7) is well behaved.

In short. Conjugate prior ⇒ posterior in the same family, updated by simple counting (Beta: add heads to α, tails to β).
Check yourselfPrior Beta(2, 2); you see 3 heads, 1 tail. Posterior?
Answer: Beta$(2+3,\,2+1)$ = Beta(5, 3), with mean $5/8=0.625$.

6.7Change of Variables / Inverse Transform

If $X$ has a known distribution and $Y=U(X)$, what is the distribution of $Y$? Careful: you cannot just plug $U^{-1}(y)$ into $p_X$. Stretching the axis thins out the probability, and the density must be corrected by how much. The recipe for an invertible $U$:

$$p_Y(y)=p_X\big(U^{-1}(y)\big)\cdot\Big|\frac{d}{dy}U^{-1}(y)\Big|$$

(Derived from the CDF: $F_Y(y)=F_X(U^{-1}(y))$ and then differentiate with the chain rule.) In several dimensions the correction factor becomes $|\det J|$ of the inverse map, the Jacobian from Chapter 5, which is why that determinant mattered.

Inverse transform sampling

The "$Y=\Phi(X)$" preset shows the probability integral transform (book Thm 6.15): feed any continuous variable through its own CDF and you get Uniform(0,1). Reverse it and you can generate samples from any distribution whose CDF you can invert: draw a uniform number $u$ (what computers provide) and output $F^{-1}(u)$.

Going deeper: two techniques, a worked example, and the multivariate version

Discrete case (easy): for invertible $U$, $P(Y=y)=P(X=U^{-1}(y))$. Probabilities just get relabelled.

Technique 1: the distribution-function method

(1) Find the CDF of $Y$: $F_Y(y)=P(Y\le y)$. (2) Differentiate: $f_Y(y)=\frac{d}{dy}F_Y(y)$. Mind the new range of $Y$.

Book Ex. 6.16 $Y=X^2$ with $f_X(x)=3x^2$ on $[0,1]$

$F_X(x)=x^3$. $F_Y(y)=P(X^2\le y)=P(X\le\sqrt y)=F_X(\sqrt y)=y^{3/2}$ for $0\le y\le1$. Differentiate: $f_Y(y)=\tfrac32\sqrt y$. Via the change-of-variable recipe: $U^{-1}(y)=\sqrt y$, $f_X(\sqrt y)=3y$, $\big|\frac{d}{dy}\sqrt y\big|=\frac1{2\sqrt y}$, product $\frac{3y}{2\sqrt y}=\tfrac32\sqrt y$ ✓ (same answer).

Technique 2: change of variables, and why the factor appears

It is the substitution rule of integration in disguise: $\int f(g(x))g'(x)dx=\int f(u)du$. Starting from $F_Y(y)=F_X(U^{-1}(y))$ (for increasing $U$), differentiating with the chain rule gives $f_Y(y)=f_X(U^{-1}(y))\frac{d}{dy}U^{-1}(y)$. For a decreasing $U$ the same derivation gives a minus sign, so the absolute value covers both cases. The factor measures "how much a unit length is stretched by $U^{-1}$". (Probability mass in a small piece $dx$ is conserved: $f_Y(y)\,|dy|=f_X(x)\,|dx|$.)

Thm 6.16Multivariate change of variables

If $\mathbf y=U(\mathbf x)$ is differentiable and invertible, then $f(\mathbf y)=f_{\mathbf x}\big(U^{-1}(\mathbf y)\big)\cdot\Big|\det\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf y}U^{-1}(\mathbf y)\Big|$. Same recipe: invert, substitute, multiply by the absolute Jacobian determinant (Chapter 5: the polar-coordinates widget showed $|\det J|=r$).

Book Ex. 6.17 A linear map of a standard bivariate Gaussian

$f(\mathbf x)=\frac1{2\pi}\exp(-\frac12\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x)$ and $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x$, $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Inverse: $\mathbf x=A^{-1}\mathbf y$, Jacobian $\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf y}A^{-1}\mathbf y=A^{-1}$ with determinant $\frac1{ad-bc}$. So

$$f(\mathbf y)=\frac1{2\pi}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12\mathbf y^\top A^{-\top}A^{-1}\mathbf y\big)\,|ad-bc|^{-1},$$

a bivariate Gaussian with covariance $AA^\top$, confirming "linear maps of Gaussians are Gaussian".

Thm 6.15Probability integral transform

If $X$ is continuous with a strictly increasing CDF $F_X$, then $Y=F_X(X)$ is uniform on $[0,1]$. (Exercise E6.13.) Used backwards ($X=F_X^{-1}(U)$) it is inverse transform sampling: it generates samples from any distribution with an invertible CDF using only uniform random numbers.

In short. $p_Y(y)=p_X(U^{-1}(y))\,|dU^{-1}/dy|$. The extra factor accounts for stretching. In many dimensions it's the Jacobian determinant.
Check yourself$X\sim$ Uniform(0,1) and $Y=3X$. What is the density of $Y$ on $[0,3]$?
Answer: $p_Y(y)=1\cdot|d(y/3)/dy|=1/3$: spread over three times the length, so one third as tall.

E6.2 · Mean, mode and median of a mixture book 6.2

$p(\mathbf x)=0.4\,\mathcal N\big(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}10\\2\end{smallmatrix}\right],I\big)+0.6\,\mathcal N\big(\left[\begin{smallmatrix}0\\0\end{smallmatrix}\right],\left[\begin{smallmatrix}8.4&2\\2&1.7\end{smallmatrix}\right]\big)$. (a) Marginals. (b) Mean, mode, median of each marginal. (c) Mean and mode of the 2D distribution.

Solution

(a) Marginals of a Gaussian are read off the mean and the diagonal of the covariance, and marginalising a mixture marginalises each component: $p(x_1)=0.4\,\mathcal N(x_1\mid10,1)+0.6\,\mathcal N(x_1\mid0,8.4)$ and $p(x_2)=0.4\,\mathcal N(x_2\mid2,1)+0.6\,\mathcal N(x_2\mid0,1.7)$.

(b) Means: $0.4\cdot10+0.6\cdot0=\mathbf 4$ and $0.4\cdot2+0=\mathbf{0.8}$. Medians (solve $F(m)=0.5$ numerically): $x_1$: $\approx\mathbf{2.80}$; $x_2$: $\approx\mathbf{0.87}$. Modes (peak of the density, numerically): $x_1$: $\approx\mathbf{10}$ (a second, lower peak near 0); $x_2$: $\approx\mathbf{1.33}$ (single peak, between the two component means).

(c) 2D mean: $(4,\ 0.8)$. 2D mode: the global peak is the first component's peak, $\approx(10,2)$ (height $\approx0.064$), versus a lower local peak at $(0,0)$ (height $\approx0.030$). Note the mean $(4,0.8)$ sits in a region of low density, and "the median of a 2D distribution" has no natural definition.

E6.3 · Conjugate prior for a Bernoulli book 6.3

A program compiles ($x=1$) or not ($x=0$) with $p(x\mid\mu)=\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}$. Choose a conjugate prior and compute $p(\mu\mid x_1,\dots,x_N)$.

Solution

The Beta distribution is conjugate to the Bernoulli. Prior $\mu\sim\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)\propto\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}$. For i.i.d. observations with $s=\sum_nx_n$ successes, the likelihood is $\prod_n\mu^{x_n}(1-\mu)^{1-x_n}=\mu^s(1-\mu)^{N-s}$. Bayes: posterior $\propto\mu^{s+\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{N-s+\beta-1}$, i.e.

$$p(\mu\mid x_1,\dots,x_N)=\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha+s,\ \beta+N-s),\qquad\mathbb{E}[\mu\mid\text{data}]=\frac{\alpha+s}{\alpha+\beta+N}.$$

Counts of successes and failures are simply added to $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

E6.4 · Mangos and bags book 6.4

Bag 1: 4 mangos, 2 apples. Bag 2: 4 mangos, 4 apples. A biased coin shows heads with probability $0.6$ (then draw from bag 1) else tails $0.4$ (bag 2). You are handed a mango. What is the probability it came from bag 2?

Solution

$P(\text{bag 1})=0.6$, $P(\text{bag 2})=0.4$, $P(M\mid1)=\tfrac46=\tfrac23$, $P(M\mid2)=\tfrac48=\tfrac12$. Evidence: $P(M)=0.6\cdot\tfrac23+0.4\cdot\tfrac12=0.4+0.2=0.6$. Bayes:

$$P(\text{bag 2}\mid M)=\frac{P(M\mid2)P(\text{bag 2})}{P(M)}=\frac{\frac12\cdot0.4}{0.6}=\frac{0.2}{0.6}=\frac13.$$

Bag 2 is a priori less likely (0.4) and has fewer mangos in proportion, so the mango points toward bag 1.

E6.5 · A linear-Gaussian time series (the Kalman filter) book 6.5

$\mathbf x_{t+1}=A\mathbf x_t+\mathbf w$, $\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)$; $\mathbf y_t=C\mathbf x_t+\mathbf v$, $\mathbf v\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$; $p(\mathbf x_0)=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_0,\Sigma_0)$; noises independent. (a) What is the form of $p(\mathbf x_0,\dots,\mathbf x_T)$? (b) Assume $p(\mathbf x_t\mid\mathbf y_{1:t})=\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_t,\Sigma_t)$. Compute (1) $p(\mathbf x_{t+1}\mid\mathbf y_{1:t})$, (2) $p(\mathbf x_{t+1},\mathbf y_{t+1}\mid\mathbf y_{1:t})$, (3) $p(\mathbf x_{t+1}\mid\mathbf y_{1:t+1})$ when $\mathbf y_{t+1}=\hat{\mathbf y}$ is observed.

Solution

(a) By the product rule $p(\mathbf x_0,\dots,\mathbf x_T)=p(\mathbf x_0)\prod_{t}p(\mathbf x_{t+1}\mid\mathbf x_t)$ with each factor $\mathcal N(\mathbf x_{t+1}\mid A\mathbf x_t,Q)$. Every $\mathbf x_t$ is a linear function of the independent Gaussians $\mathbf x_0,\mathbf w_0,\mathbf w_1,\dots$, and linear maps of Gaussians are Gaussian, so the joint is a (multivariate) Gaussian.

(b1) Prediction: $\mathbf x_{t+1}=A\mathbf x_t+\mathbf w$ gives $p(\mathbf x_{t+1}\mid\mathbf y_{1:t})=\mathcal N(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu},\bar\Sigma)$ with $\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}=A\boldsymbol\mu_t$, $\bar\Sigma=A\Sigma_tA^\top+Q$.

(b2) With $\mathbf y_{t+1}=C\mathbf x_{t+1}+\mathbf v$: jointly Gaussian with mean $(\bar{\boldsymbol\mu},\,C\bar{\boldsymbol\mu})$ and covariance $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\bar\Sigma&\bar\Sigma C^\top\\C\bar\Sigma&C\bar\Sigma C^\top+R\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

(b3) Condition on $\mathbf y_{t+1}=\hat{\mathbf y}$ with the Gaussian conditioning rule:

$$\boldsymbol\mu_{t+1}=\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}+\bar\Sigma C^\top(C\bar\Sigma C^\top+R)^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf y}-C\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}),\qquad\Sigma_{t+1}=\bar\Sigma-\bar\Sigma C^\top(C\bar\Sigma C^\top+R)^{-1}C\bar\Sigma.$$

This predict-then-correct loop is the Kalman filter. The term $\hat{\mathbf y}-C\bar{\boldsymbol\mu}$ is the "surprise", and the matrix $\bar\Sigma C^\top(\cdot)^{-1}$ (the Kalman gain) decides how much of it to believe.

E6.6 · The raw-score formula book 6.6

Prove $\mathbb{V}[X]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-(\mathbb{E}[X])^2$.

Solution

With $\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]$ and linearity of expectation: $\mathbb{V}[X]=\mathbb{E}[(X-\mu)^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2-2\mu X+\mu^2]=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\mu\,\mathbb{E}[X]+\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-2\mu^2+\mu^2=\mathbb{E}[X^2]-\mu^2$. ∎ (Same with sample means for the empirical version.)

E6.7 · Variance as pairwise differences book 6.7

Prove $\dfrac1{N^2}\sum_{i,j=1}^N(x_i-x_j)^2=2\Big(\dfrac1N\sum_ix_i^2-\big(\dfrac1N\sum_ix_i\big)^2\Big)$.

Solution

Expand the square: $(x_i-x_j)^2=x_i^2-2x_ix_j+x_j^2$. Summing over all $N^2$ pairs: $\sum_{i,j}x_i^2=N\sum_ix_i^2$, likewise $\sum_{i,j}x_j^2=N\sum_jx_j^2$, and $\sum_{i,j}x_ix_j=\big(\sum_ix_i\big)^2$. So $\dfrac1{N^2}\Big[2N\sum x_i^2-2\big(\sum x_i\big)^2\Big]=2\Big(\dfrac1N\sum x_i^2-\big(\dfrac1N\sum x_i\big)^2\Big)$. ∎ The right side is twice the raw-score variance: the average squared distance between pairs is twice the variance. (Checked numerically on random data.)

E6.8 · Bernoulli in exponential-family form book 6.8

Write $p(x\mid\mu)=\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}$ as $h(x)\exp(\theta\,\phi(x)-A(\theta))$.

Solution

$\mu^x(1-\mu)^{1-x}=\exp\big[x\log\mu+(1-x)\log(1-\mu)\big]=\exp\big[x\log\tfrac{\mu}{1-\mu}+\log(1-\mu)\big]$. So $h(x)=1$, $\phi(x)=x$, natural parameter $\theta=\log\frac\mu{1-\mu}$, and $A(\theta)=-\log(1-\mu)=\log(1+e^\theta)$ (since $\mu=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\theta}}$).

E6.9 · Binomial and Beta as exponential families book 6.9

Express the Binomial and the Beta distributions in exponential-family form and show their product is again of that form.

Solution

Binomial: $\binom Nx\mu^x(1-\mu)^{N-x}=\binom Nx\exp\big[x\theta-N\log(1+e^\theta)\big]$ with $\theta=\log\frac\mu{1-\mu}$: $h(x)=\binom Nx$, $\phi(x)=x$, $A(\theta)=N\log(1+e^\theta)$.

Beta: $p(\mu\mid\alpha,\beta)=\exp\big[(\alpha-1)\log\mu+(\beta-1)\log(1-\mu)-\log B(\alpha,\beta)\big]$ with $B(\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha+\beta)}$: $h=1$, $\boldsymbol\phi(\mu)=(\log\mu,\log(1-\mu))$, $\boldsymbol\theta=(\alpha-1,\beta-1)$, $A=\log B(\alpha,\beta)$.

Product (as a function of $\mu$): $\mu^x(1-\mu)^{N-x}\cdot\mu^{\alpha-1}(1-\mu)^{\beta-1}=\exp\big[(\alpha+x-1)\log\mu+(\beta+N-x-1)\log(1-\mu)\big]$: the same sufficient statistics $(\log\mu,\log(1-\mu))$ with natural parameters $(\alpha+x-1,\ \beta+N-x-1)$, i.e. an (unnormalised) member of the family, namely a Beta$(\alpha+x,\beta+N-x)$. This is conjugacy seen through natural parameters: the natural parameters simply add.

E6.10 · The product of two Gaussians book 6.10

Show $\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf a,A)\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf b,B)=c\,\mathcal N(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf c,C)$ with $C=(A^{-1}+B^{-1})^{-1}$, $\mathbf c=C(A^{-1}\mathbf a+B^{-1}\mathbf b)$, $c=\mathcal N(\mathbf a\mid\mathbf b,A+B)$. (a) by completing the square, (b) via exponential-family form.

Solution

(a) Multiply the densities; the exponent is $-\tfrac12\big[(\mathbf x-\mathbf a)^\top A^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf a)+(\mathbf x-\mathbf b)^\top B^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf b)\big]=-\tfrac12\big[\mathbf x^\top(A^{-1}+B^{-1})\mathbf x-2\mathbf x^\top(A^{-1}\mathbf a+B^{-1}\mathbf b)+\mathbf a^\top A^{-1}\mathbf a+\mathbf b^\top B^{-1}\mathbf b\big]$. With $C^{-1}=A^{-1}+B^{-1}$ and $\mathbf c=C(A^{-1}\mathbf a+B^{-1}\mathbf b)$ the $\mathbf x$-terms become $(\mathbf x-\mathbf c)^\top C^{-1}(\mathbf x-\mathbf c)-\mathbf c^\top C^{-1}\mathbf c$. The leftover constant $\mathbf a^\top A^{-1}\mathbf a+\mathbf b^\top B^{-1}\mathbf b-\mathbf c^\top C^{-1}\mathbf c$ simplifies (matrix identities) to $(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)^\top(A+B)^{-1}(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)$, which together with the determinant identity $|A+B|=|A||B||C^{-1}|$ gives $c=(2\pi)^{-D/2}|A+B|^{-1/2}\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)^\top(A+B)^{-1}(\mathbf a-\mathbf b)\big)=\mathcal N(\mathbf a\mid\mathbf b,A+B)$.

(b) A Gaussian is $\propto\exp(\boldsymbol\theta^\top\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x))$ with $\boldsymbol\phi(\mathbf x)=(\mathbf x,\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top)$ and natural parameters $(\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu,\ -\tfrac12\Sigma^{-1})$. Multiplying exponential-family densities with the same $\boldsymbol\phi$ adds the natural parameters: $\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu=A^{-1}\mathbf a+B^{-1}\mathbf b$ and $\Sigma^{-1}=A^{-1}+B^{-1}$, i.e. exactly $C$ and $\mathbf c$ above.

Numeric check (1D, $a=0,A=1,b=2,B=3$): both sides equal $0.0472$ at $x=1$. A multivariate random example also matched.

E6.11 · Iterated expectations book 6.11

Show $\mathbb{E}_X[x]=\mathbb{E}_Y\big[\mathbb{E}_X[x\mid y]\big]$.

Solution

$\mathbb{E}_Y[\mathbb{E}_X[x\mid y]]=\int\Big(\int x\,p(x\mid y)\,dx\Big)p(y)\,dy=\iint x\,p(x\mid y)p(y)\,dx\,dy=\iint x\,p(x,y)\,dx\,dy=\int x\Big(\int p(x,y)dy\Big)dx=\int x\,p(x)\,dx=\mathbb{E}_X[x]$. ∎ (Product rule, then sum rule.) Intuition: the overall average is the average of the group averages, weighted by group size. This is also the source of the mixture-mean formula.

E6.12 · Gaussian manipulation (the backbone of Bayesian linear regression) book 6.12

$\mathbf x\sim\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu_x,\Sigma_x)$, $\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D$; $\mathbf y=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b+\mathbf w$ with $\mathbf w\sim\mathcal N(0,Q)$ independent of $\mathbf x$. (a) $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)$. (b) $p(\mathbf y)$. (c) For $\mathbf z=C\mathbf y+\mathbf v$, $\mathbf v\sim\mathcal N(0,R)$: $p(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf y)$ and $p(\mathbf z)$. (d) The posterior $p(\mathbf x\mid\hat{\mathbf y})$.

Solution

(a) Given $\mathbf x$, the only randomness is $\mathbf w$: $p(\mathbf y\mid\mathbf x)=\mathcal N(\mathbf y\mid A\mathbf x+\mathbf b,\ Q)$.

(b) $\mathbf y$ is a linear function of independent Gaussians, so it is Gaussian. Mean: $\boldsymbol\mu_y=A\boldsymbol\mu_x+\mathbf b+0$. Covariance: by independence of $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf w$, $\Sigma_y=A\Sigma_xA^\top+Q$. So $p(\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(A\boldsymbol\mu_x+\mathbf b,\ A\Sigma_xA^\top+Q)$.

(c) $p(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf y)=\mathcal N(C\mathbf y,R)$, and by the same argument $\boldsymbol\mu_z=C\boldsymbol\mu_y=C(A\boldsymbol\mu_x+\mathbf b)$, $\Sigma_z=C\Sigma_yC^\top+R=C(A\Sigma_xA^\top+Q)C^\top+R$.

(d) The cross-covariance is $\operatorname{Cov}[\mathbf x,\mathbf y]=\mathbb{E}[(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_x)(A(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_x)+\mathbf w)^\top]=\Sigma_xA^\top$ (the noise term vanishes). So $(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ is jointly Gaussian with blocks $\Sigma_x$, $\Sigma_xA^\top$, $\Sigma_y$ and the conditioning rule gives

$$p(\mathbf x\mid\hat{\mathbf y})=\mathcal N\Big(\boldsymbol\mu_x+\Sigma_xA^\top(A\Sigma_xA^\top+Q)^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf y}-A\boldsymbol\mu_x-\mathbf b),\ \ \Sigma_x-\Sigma_xA^\top(A\Sigma_xA^\top+Q)^{-1}A\Sigma_x\Big).$$

Equivalent "precision form" (verified numerically): posterior precision $=\Sigma_x^{-1}+A^\top Q^{-1}A$: prior precision plus data precision. With $\mathbf x$ as regression weights, this is Bayesian linear regression (Chapter 9).

E6.13 · The probability integral transform book 6.13

For a continuous $X$ with strictly increasing CDF $F_X$, show $Y:=F_X(X)$ is uniformly distributed on $[0,1]$.

Solution

$Y\in[0,1]$. For $y\in[0,1]$: $P(Y\le y)=P(F_X(X)\le y)=P\big(X\le F_X^{-1}(y)\big)$ (apply the increasing function $F_X^{-1}$ to both sides) $=F_X\big(F_X^{-1}(y)\big)=y$. A CDF equal to $y$ on $[0,1]$ is the CDF of the uniform distribution. ∎ Reverse it to sample: draw $U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ and output $F_X^{-1}(U)$. (Try "Y = Φ(X) → uniform" in the change-of-variables widget.)
