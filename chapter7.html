Chapter 7 · Continuous Optimization

Training a model = finding the best parameters. This chapter is how you actually walk there.

Why this chapter?

Almost every ML method ends with "...and then we minimise a loss". That is optimisation. It uses everything before: gradients (Ch. 5) point downhill, Hessians (Ch. 5) measure curvature, eigenvalues (Ch. 4) explain why some valleys are hard, and probability (Ch. 6) explains why noisy gradients still work.

7.1Optimization Using Gradient Descent

We want $\min_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)$ for a differentiable $f$ (often there is no closed-form solution). Imagine a ball on a hilly surface. The gradient points uphill, so step against it:

$$\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i-\gamma_i\,(\nabla f)(\mathbf x_i)^\top$$

$\gamma$ is the step size (the learning rate). For a small enough $\gamma$, $f$ never increases. The path ends at a local minimum, and which one you reach depends on where you start. Here's a function with two valleys (this isn't the book's picture, just an illustration):

Starting right of the hill you roll into the shallow valley. Starting left you reach the deep one. Too small a $\gamma$ is slow, too large a $\gamma$ overshoots and can blow up. For general non-convex $f$, gradient descent promises a local minimum, not the best one.

The step size matters

The book gives two simple heuristics: if $f$ went up after a step, the step was too large (undo it and shrink $\gamma$). If $f$ went down, you could try a bigger step. Adaptive methods automate this.

Poorly conditioned problems: the zig-zag

Book Example 7.1: $f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{bmatrix}\mathbf x-\begin{bmatrix}5\\3\end{bmatrix}^\top\mathbf x$, starting at $(-3,-1)$ with $\gamma=0.085$. The surface is a long thin valley: very steep in $x_2$, very flat in $x_1$. The ratio of steepest to flattest curvature is the condition number $\kappa$ (the ratio of largest to smallest singular value, Ch. 4). Large $\kappa$ ⇒ gradient descent zig-zags across the valley and crawls along it.

Momentum: give the ball some memory

Add a fraction $\alpha\in[0,1]$ of the previous step to the new one (a heavy ball that resists changing direction):

$$\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i-\gamma_i(\nabla f)(\mathbf x_i)^\top+\alpha\,\Delta\mathbf x_i,\qquad\Delta\mathbf x_i=\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_{i-1}$$

It averages out the zig-zag (the opposite jumps cancel) and builds speed along the valley. Try the "With momentum" preset and compare how many steps it needs.

Stochastic gradient descent (SGD)

ML losses are sums over data points: $L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^NL_n(\boldsymbol\theta)$. The full gradient needs all $N$ points, which is expensive. SGD estimates it from a random mini-batch (even one example). The estimate is noisy but unbiased: on average it equals the true gradient, and that's all convergence needs. The "Noisy gradient" preset mimics this by adding random noise to each gradient:

  • Large batches: accurate gradient, smooth path, but expensive per step.
  • Small batches: cheap, noisy, and the noise can even help escape bad local minima.
  • With a suitably decreasing learning rate, SGD converges to a local minimum (book remark).

ExampleBook Example 7.2: solving $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ by descent

Minimise $\|A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|^2$. The gradient is $2(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)^\top A$. In this special case you can set the gradient to zero and solve directly (Ch. 9), but gradient descent also works, with speed governed by the condition number $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}(A)/\sigma_{\min}(A)$. A preconditioner $P^{-1}$ tries to make the valley rounder.

Going deeper: why gradient descent works, step-size rules, conditioning, SGD in detail

Why a small step decreases $f$

By the first-order Taylor expansion (Chapter 5), $f(\mathbf x-\gamma\nabla f^\top)\approx f(\mathbf x)-\gamma\,\|\nabla f\|^2$. The decrease is positive whenever $\nabla f\ne0$ and $\gamma$ is small enough that the approximation holds. When the gradient is zero you are at a stationary point (minimum, maximum or saddle); the Hessian tells which (Chapter 5.7). Gradient descent never "knows" if it found the global minimum, only that it is going downhill. For convex $f$ (7.3) that is enough.

Step-size heuristics (book, after Toussaint)

  • If $f$ increased after a step, $\gamma$ was too large: undo the step and decrease $\gamma$ (this wastes one step but guarantees monotone decrease).
  • If $f$ decreased, try a slightly larger $\gamma$ next time.

Adaptive methods automate rules like these. For SGD, convergence needs a step size that shrinks at a suitable rate.

Book Ex. 7.1 Reproducing the book's first steps

$f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&20\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mathbf x-\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5\\3\end{smallmatrix}\right]^\top\mathbf x$, so $\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^\top Q-\mathbf b^\top$. At $\mathbf x_0=(-3,-1)$: $\nabla f=(-3,-1)Q-(5,3)=(-7,-23)-(5,3)=(-12,-26)$. With $\gamma=0.085$: $\mathbf x_1=(-3,-1)-0.085(-12,-26)=(-1.98,\ 1.21)$ ✓ (the book's value). Notice $x_2$ jumped from $-1$ to $+1.21$: it overshot the narrow valley, exactly the zig-zag the widget shows.

Why $\gamma\approx0.1$ is the stability limit: the curvature along each eigen-direction of $Q$ is its eigenvalue ($\approx1.95$ and $\approx20.05$, Chapter 4). Plain gradient descent on a quadratic converges iff $\gamma\lt2/\lambda_{\max}\approx0.0997$.

Conditioning and preconditioning (book Ex. 7.2)

To solve $A\mathbf x=\mathbf b$ approximately one minimises $\|A\mathbf x-\mathbf b\|^2$, whose gradient is $2(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)^\top A$. The speed of gradient descent depends on the condition number $\kappa=\sigma_{\max}(A)/\sigma_{\min}(A)$ (ratio of the most to the least curved direction): big $\kappa$ = long thin valley. A preconditioner $P^{-1}$ solves $P^{-1}(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=\mathbf 0$ instead, chosen so that $P^{-1}A$ has a smaller $\kappa$ while $P^{-1}$ is cheap to compute. (In this special case there is also the closed-form solution from setting the gradient to zero; see Chapter 9.)

Momentum, written out

$\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i-\gamma_i\nabla f(\mathbf x_i)^\top+\alpha\Delta\mathbf x_i$ with $\Delta\mathbf x_i=\mathbf x_i-\mathbf x_{i-1}=\alpha\Delta\mathbf x_{i-1}-\gamma_{i-1}\nabla f(\mathbf x_{i-1})^\top$. Unrolling shows the update is a moving average of past gradients: oscillating components cancel, consistent components add up. It also smooths noisy gradient estimates, which is why it pairs well with SGD.

SGD in detail

Typical objective: $L(\boldsymbol\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^NL_n(\boldsymbol\theta)$, e.g. negative log-likelihood $-\sum\log p(y_n\mid\mathbf x_n,\boldsymbol\theta)$. Batch gradient descent uses $\boldsymbol\theta_{i+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_i-\gamma_i\sum_{n=1}^N\nabla L_n(\boldsymbol\theta_i)^\top$. Mini-batch: replace the sum by a sum over a random subset $B$ (rescaled); one example: $\boldsymbol\theta_{i+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_i-\gamma_i\nabla L_n(\boldsymbol\theta_i)^\top$ for a randomly chosen $n$ (exercise E7.2). Convergence needs only that the estimate is an unbiased estimate of the true gradient, the way a sample mean estimates an expectation (Chapter 6). Large batches give accurate but costly steps (and use fast matrix operations); small batches are cheap and noisy, and the noise can help escape poor local optima. Goal reminder: in ML we want good generalisation, not an exact minimum of the training loss, so approximate gradients are usually fine.

In short. Gradient descent: $x\leftarrow x-\gamma\nabla f$. Small γ = slow, large γ = unstable. Thin valleys zig-zag. Momentum smooths it. SGD trades accuracy for cheapness using mini-batches.

7.2Constrained Optimization and Lagrange Multipliers

Often the answer isn't allowed to be anywhere. For example, probabilities must be $\ge0$ and add to 1. The constrained problem:

$$\min_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_i(\mathbf x)\le0\ \ (i=1,\dots,m)$$

Equality constraints $h_j(\mathbf x)=0$ are allowed too (they're two inequalities). The unconstrained minimum may simply be infeasible, so the answer is pushed to the boundary.

The Lagrangian

Instead of an "infinite wall" penalty (the book's indicator function, impossible to optimise), add each constraint with a weight $\lambda_i\ge0$, a Lagrange multiplier:

$$\mathfrak{L}(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda)=f(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf g(\mathbf x),\qquad \boldsymbol\lambda\ge0$$

(For equality constraints the multipliers are unconstrained in sign.) The geometric picture is the most useful thing to remember. Below, $f$ is "distance to $p$" and the red curve is the constraint. Slide along the constraint:

At the best feasible point, the contour of $f$ just touches the constraint curve, so $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$ are parallel: $\nabla f=-\lambda\nabla g$. The multiplier $\lambda$ says how much the optimum would improve if you relaxed the constraint a little.

Duality

Turn the problem around. The dual function is $\mathfrak{D}(\boldsymbol\lambda)=\min_{\mathbf x}\mathfrak L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda)$, and the dual problem is $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)$. The original is the primal problem. Two facts from the book:

  • Weak duality: dual value ≤ primal value, always (it follows from the minimax inequality $\max_y\min_x\varphi\le\min_x\max_y\varphi$).
  • $\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)$ is always concave, even if $f$ is not convex, so the dual is easy to maximise. For convex problems the gap closes: strong duality.

Going deeper: from "infinite wall" to Lagrangian, and weak duality, step by step

  1. Hard constraints as an infinite penalty. Define $J(\mathbf x)=f(\mathbf x)+\sum_i\mathbb 1(g_i(\mathbf x))$ with $\mathbb 1(z)=0$ for $z\le0$ and $\infty$ otherwise. Minimising $J$ is the constrained problem, but a step function is as hard to optimise as the original.
  2. Relax the wall to a ramp. Replace $\mathbb 1(g_i)$ by the linear function $\lambda_ig_i$ with $\lambda_i\ge0$: this gives the Lagrangian $\mathfrak L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda)=f(\mathbf x)+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf g(\mathbf x)$. For $\lambda_i\ge0$ it is a lower bound of $J$, and $J(\mathbf x)=\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda)$ (if some $g_i\gt0$, the max over $\lambda_i$ is $+\infty$).
  3. Primal and dual. Primal: $\min_{\mathbf x}\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak L$. Swap the order to get the dual: $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\min_{\mathbf x}\mathfrak L=\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)$, with $\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)=\min_{\mathbf x}\mathfrak L(\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\lambda)$ (the inner problem is unconstrained!).
  4. Weak duality from the minimax inequality. For any $\varphi(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$: $\max_y\min_x\varphi\le\min_x\max_y\varphi$. Proof: for all $x,y$, $\min_x\varphi(x,y)\le\varphi(x,y)\le\max_y\varphi(x,y)$; take $\max_y$ on the left, then $\min_x$ on the right. Hence dual value $\le$ primal value always.
  5. The dual is always concave. $\mathfrak L$ is affine in $\boldsymbol\lambda$, and $\mathfrak D$ is a pointwise minimum of affine functions, hence concave, even if $f$ and $g_i$ are not convex. Maximising a concave function is easy, so the dual is a tractable lower bound.

Recipe to derive a dual: write $\mathfrak L$, differentiate with respect to $\mathbf x$, set to zero, solve for $\mathbf x(\boldsymbol\lambda)$, substitute back to get $\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)$, and add the constraint $\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0$. Exercises E7.6–E7.8 practise this.

Equality constraints $h_j(\mathbf x)=0$ are two inequalities $h_j\le0$ and $h_j\ge0$; their multipliers end up unconstrained in sign, while inequality multipliers must be $\ge0$. A pattern you can observe in exercise E7.7: the optimal multiplier is zero for every constraint that is not active at the optimum (a standard fact).

In short. Constrained min: build $\mathfrak L=f+\lambda^\top g$. At the optimum $\nabla f=-\lambda\nabla g$ (gradients parallel). Dual problem ≤ primal problem.

7.3Convex Optimization

Some problems are special: every local minimum is the global minimum. That's a convex optimisation problem, the well-behaved corner of optimisation.

Convex sets and functions

  • A convex set: the line segment between any two of its points stays inside it ($\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y\in C$ for $0\le\theta\le1$). A disc is convex, a crescent is not.
  • A convex function: the chord between any two points on the graph lies above the graph:
    $$f(\theta\mathbf x+(1-\theta)\mathbf y)\le\theta f(\mathbf x)+(1-\theta)f(\mathbf y)$$
    (This is Jensen's inequality. A concave function is the negative of a convex one.)

Book Example 7.3 checks $x\log_2x$ at $x=2$, $y=4$, $\theta=0.5$: left side $3\log_23\approx4.75\le5$ = right side ✓ (the third preset).

Easier tests for differentiable $f$ (from the book):

  • $f$ is convex ⇔ $f(\mathbf y)\ge f(\mathbf x)+\nabla f(\mathbf x)(\mathbf y-\mathbf x)$ for all $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$ (every tangent lies below the curve; the green dashed line in the widget).
  • If twice differentiable: convex ⇔ the Hessian is positive semidefinite everywhere (the "bowl" case from Ch. 5).
  • Closure: a non-negative weighted sum of convex functions is convex (just like "sums of vectors stay in the space").

Linear programming (LP)

Everything linear:

$$\min_{\mathbf x}\ \mathbf c^\top\mathbf x\quad\text{subject to}\quad A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b$$

The feasible region is a polygon (a polytope in higher dimensions) and the objective is a tilted plane, so the optimum is always at a corner. Rotate the objective $\mathbf c$ and watch the optimal corner jump:

Book Example 7.5 (with $\mathbf c=(-5,-3)$, i.e. maximise $5x_1+3x_2$). Small note: the book's printed vector has $5$ for the third constraint but its figure legend says $x_2\le2x_1-5$; I follow the figure, and the optimum is the same either way. The dual of an LP is another LP, $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}-\mathbf b^\top\boldsymbol\lambda$ subject to $\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$. Use whichever has fewer variables.

Quadratic programming (QP)

Quadratic bowl objective, linear constraints (with $Q$ positive definite):

$$\min_{\mathbf x}\ \tfrac12\mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x+\mathbf c^\top\mathbf x\quad\text{subject to}\quad A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b$$

Book Example 7.6 puts a box ($|x_i|\le1$) around an ellipse-shaped objective. Grow and shrink the box:

ML link. Support vector machines (Ch. 12) are trained by solving a quadratic program. Linear regression with a constraint on the weights is one too.

Legendre–Fenchel transform (a glimpse)

Because a convex function is fully described by its tangent lines, it can be re-expressed in terms of slopes instead of positions. That re-expression is the convex conjugate $f^*(\mathbf s)=\sup_{\mathbf x}\big(\langle\mathbf s,\mathbf x\rangle-f(\mathbf x)\big)$, the second kind of duality in the book. It's used in advanced derivations (e.g. SVM duality); you can skip it on a first read.

Going deeper: convexity tests, LP and QP duals, and the Legendre–Fenchel transform

More on convexity

  • Epigraph: "pour water into" a convex function; the filled region $\{(\mathbf x,t):t\ge f(\mathbf x)\}$ is a convex set. That's how convex functions and convex sets are linked. Sublevel sets $\{f\le c\}$ of convex $f$ are convex, which is why constraints $g_i(\mathbf x)\le0$ with convex $g_i$ give a convex feasible region.
  • Closure: intersections of convex sets are convex; a non-negative weighted sum $\alpha f_1+\beta f_2$ ($\alpha,\beta\ge0$) of convex functions is convex (multiply the defining inequality by $\alpha$, add the two, rearrange). The maximum of convex functions is convex; differences and products need not be (exercise E7.4).
  • The defining inequality is a special case of Jensen's inequality, which extends to any non-negative weights summing to 1.

Book Ex. 7.3 Checking convexity of $f(x)=x\log_2x$

Definition with $x=2$, $y=4$, $\theta=\tfrac12$: left side $f(3)=3\log_23\approx4.75$, right side $\tfrac12f(2)+\tfrac12f(4)=\tfrac12\cdot2+\tfrac12\cdot8=5$, so $4.75\le5$ ✓. Gradient criterion: $f'(x)=\log_2x+\frac1{\ln2}$, so $f(2)+f'(2)(4-2)=2+(1+1.4427)\cdot2\approx6.89\le f(4)=8$ ✓. (Two points are a sanity check, not a proof; a proof needs all points, e.g. $f''(x)=\frac1{x\ln2}\gt0$.)

Why an LP's dual looks the way it does

Primal: $\min\mathbf c^\top\mathbf x$ s.t. $A\mathbf x\le\mathbf b$. $\mathfrak L=\mathbf c^\top\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf b$. This is linear in $\mathbf x$, so its minimum over $\mathbf x$ is $-\infty$ unless the coefficient vanishes: $\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$. Then $\mathfrak D(\boldsymbol\lambda)=-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf b$, giving the dual $\max-\mathbf b^\top\boldsymbol\lambda$ s.t. $\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf 0$: again an LP, with $m$ variables (one per primal constraint) instead of $d$. Exercise E7.6 does it for the book's example.

The QP dual

$\mathfrak L=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top Q\mathbf x+(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf b$. Setting the gradient to zero: $Q\mathbf x+\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$, so $\mathbf x=-Q^{-1}(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)$ ($Q$ positive definite, hence invertible). Substituting back:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\ -\tfrac12(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top Q^{-1}(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf b.$$

Application: the support vector machine (Chapter 12) is a QP, and often solved through its dual.

The Legendre–Fenchel transform (convex conjugate)

Idea: describe $f$ not by its values $f(\mathbf x)$ but by its tangent slopes $\mathbf s=\nabla f(\mathbf x)$. For a fixed slope $s$, among all lines $y=sx+c$ that touch the graph, the lowest intercept is $c=\inf_x\,(f(x)-sx)$. By convention the conjugate is the negative of that intercept:

$$f^*(\mathbf s)=\sup_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D}\big(\langle\mathbf s,\mathbf x\rangle-f(\mathbf x)\big).$$

It is defined for any $f$ (convex or not, differentiable or not); for convex differentiable $f$ the supremum is attained where $\nabla f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf s$ and there is a clean one-to-one correspondence; applying the transform twice returns $f$; and the slope of $f^*$ at $\mathbf s$ is the $\mathbf x$ where $f$ has slope $\mathbf s$.

Book Ex. 7.7 Conjugate of a quadratic

$f(\mathbf y)=\tfrac\lambda2\mathbf y^\top K^{-1}\mathbf y$ ($K$ positive definite). The gradient of $\langle\mathbf y,\boldsymbol\alpha\rangle-f(\mathbf y)$ is $(\boldsymbol\alpha-\lambda K^{-1}\mathbf y)^\top$, zero at $\mathbf y=\tfrac1\lambda K\boldsymbol\alpha$. Substituting: $f^*(\boldsymbol\alpha)=\tfrac1\lambda\boldsymbol\alpha^\top K\boldsymbol\alpha-\tfrac\lambda2\cdot\tfrac1{\lambda^2}\boldsymbol\alpha^\top K\boldsymbol\alpha=\dfrac1{2\lambda}\boldsymbol\alpha^\top K\boldsymbol\alpha$. (A scaled inverse matrix flips: $K^{-1}\leftrightarrow K$.)

Book Ex. 7.8 Sums of separable losses

For $L(\mathbf t)=\sum_i\ell_i(t_i)$, $L^*(\mathbf z)=\sup_{\mathbf t}\sum_i(z_it_i-\ell_i(t_i))=\sum_i\ell_i^*(z_i)$: the conjugate of a sum over independent coordinates is the sum of conjugates. That's why conjugates are convenient for training losses that add up over examples.

Book Ex. 7.9 Duality via conjugates

For convex $f,g$ and matrix $A$: $\min_{\mathbf x}f(A\mathbf x)+g(\mathbf x)=\min_{A\mathbf x=\mathbf y}f(\mathbf y)+g(\mathbf x)$. Introduce a multiplier $\mathbf u$ for $A\mathbf x=\mathbf y$, swap min and max (allowed by convexity), split into the $\mathbf y$-part and the $\mathbf x$-part, and recognise conjugates:

$$\min_{\mathbf x}f(A\mathbf x)+g(\mathbf x)=\max_{\mathbf u}\ -f^*(\mathbf u)-g^*(-A^\top\mathbf u).$$

Strong duality (primal = dual) holds because the problem is convex. This is the template used to derive duals of regularised loss minimisation (e.g. SVMs).

In short. Convex = chord above curve = every local min is global. LP: corners. QP: bowl + linear constraints. Test convexity with tangents or a PSD Hessian.

E7.1 · Stationary points book 7.1

Find the stationary points of $f(x)=x^3+6x^2-3x-5$ and classify them.

Solution

$f'(x)=3x^2+12x-3=3(x^2+4x-1)=0\Rightarrow x=-2\pm\sqrt5$. Second derivative $f''(x)=6x+12$:

  • $x=-2+\sqrt5\approx0.236$: $f''=6\sqrt5\gt0$, a local minimum, $f\approx-5.36$.
  • $x=-2-\sqrt5\approx-4.236$: $f''=-6\sqrt5\lt0$, a local maximum, $f\approx39.36$.

No saddle points (a saddle would need $f'=f''=0$ at the same point; $f''(-2)=0$ is the inflection point, where $f'\ne0$). Neither is global: $f\to\pm\infty$ at the ends.

E7.2 · SGD with mini-batch size one book 7.2

Write the SGD update of eq. (7.15) for a mini-batch of size one.

Solution

Draw an index $n$ uniformly at random from $\{1,\dots,N\}$ and update with the gradient of that single term only:

$$\boldsymbol\theta_{i+1}=\boldsymbol\theta_i-\gamma_i\big(\nabla L_n(\boldsymbol\theta_i)\big)^\top.$$

(Batch GD would use $\sum_{n=1}^N\nabla L_n$; the single-example gradient is an unbiased but noisy stand-in for it, up to a factor $N$ absorbed in $\gamma_i$.)

E7.3 · Convex sets: true or false? book 7.3

(a) The intersection of any two convex sets is convex. (b) The union of any two convex sets is convex. (c) The difference $A\setminus B$ of convex sets is convex.

Solution

(a) True. If $\mathbf x,\mathbf y$ lie in both sets, the segment between them lies in each set, hence in the intersection.

(b) False. $A=[0,1]$ and $B=[2,3]$ are convex, but the union contains $0$ and $3$ and not their midpoint $1.5$.

(c) False. $A=[0,3]$, $B=[1,2]$: $A\setminus B=[0,1)\cup(2,3]$ is not convex (it misses the midpoint $1.5$ of $0.5$ and $2.5$).

E7.4 · Convex functions: true or false? book 7.4

(a) The sum of two convex functions is convex. (b) The difference. (c) The product. (d) The maximum.

Solution

(a) True (add the two defining inequalities). (b) False: $f=0$ and $g=x^2$ are convex, but $f-g=-x^2$ is concave. (c) False: $f(x)=x$ and $g(x)=-x$ are convex (linear), but $fg=-x^2$ is not. (d) True: $\max(f,g)(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\max\{\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y),\ \theta g(x)+(1-\theta)g(y)\}\le\theta\max(f,g)(x)+(1-\theta)\max(f,g)(y)$.

E7.5 · Write as a standard LP book 7.5

Express $\max_{\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^2,\ \xi\in\mathbb{R}}\ \mathbf p^\top\mathbf x+\xi$ subject to $\xi\ge0$, $x_0\le0$, $x_1\le3$ as a standard LP $\min\mathbf c^\top\mathbf z$ s.t. $A\mathbf z\le\mathbf b$.

Solution

Stack the variables as $\mathbf z=(x_0,x_1,\xi)$. Maximising is minimising the negative: $\mathbf c=-(p_0,\,p_1,\,1)$. Write each constraint as "$\le$":

$$\min_{\mathbf z\in\mathbb{R}^3}-\begin{bmatrix}p_0\\p_1\\1\end{bmatrix}^{\!\top}\mathbf z\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&0&-1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{bmatrix}}_{A}\mathbf z\le\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\3\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf b}$$

(row 1 is $-\xi\le0$, i.e. $\xi\ge0$). Side remark: as stated, $\xi$ has no upper bound, so this LP is unbounded (objective $\to-\infty$ as $\xi\to\infty$). It is still a valid formulation, which is all the exercise asks.

E7.6 · The dual of the book's linear program book 7.6

Derive the dual of $\min_{\mathbf x}-\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5\\3\end{smallmatrix}\right]^\top\mathbf x$ s.t. $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&2\\2&-4\\-2&1\\0&-1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mathbf x\le\left[\begin{smallmatrix}33\\8\\5\\-1\\8\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

Solution

Lagrangian with $\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^5_{\ge0}$: $\mathfrak L=\mathbf c^\top\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\lambda^\top(A\mathbf x-\mathbf b)=(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf b$. Minimising a linear function over free $\mathbf x$ is $-\infty$ unless $\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\mathbf 0$. So

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^5}\ -\mathbf b^\top\boldsymbol\lambda\quad\text{s.t.}\quad A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=\begin{bmatrix}5\\3\end{bmatrix},\ \ \boldsymbol\lambda\ge\mathbf 0,$$

explicitly: maximise $-(33\lambda_1+8\lambda_2+5\lambda_3-\lambda_4+8\lambda_5)$ s.t. $2\lambda_1+2\lambda_2-2\lambda_3=5$, $\ 2\lambda_1-4\lambda_2+\lambda_3-\lambda_4+\lambda_5=3$, $\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0$.

Check of strong duality: the dual optimum is $\boldsymbol\lambda=(\tfrac{13}6,\tfrac13,0,0,0)$ (solve the two equations with only $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ nonzero), giving dual value $-(33\cdot\tfrac{13}6+8\cdot\tfrac13)=-74.17$, equal to the primal optimum $-74.17$ at $\mathbf x^*=(12.33,4.17)$. Only the two constraints active at the optimum have nonzero multipliers. (Solved with an LP solver too.)

E7.7 · The dual of the book's quadratic program book 7.7

Derive the dual of $\min\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&4\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mathbf x+\left[\begin{smallmatrix}5\\3\end{smallmatrix}\right]^\top\mathbf x$ s.t. $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\-1&0\\0&1\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right]\mathbf x\le\mathbf 1$.

Solution

With $Q=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&4\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, $\mathbf c=(5,3)$, the general QP dual (derived in 7.3) is $\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}-\tfrac12(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)^\top Q^{-1}(\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda)-\boldsymbol\lambda^\top\mathbf 1$. Here $Q^{-1}=\tfrac17\left[\begin{smallmatrix}4&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $\mathbf c+A^\top\boldsymbol\lambda=(5+\lambda_1-\lambda_2,\ 3+\lambda_3-\lambda_4)$. With $u=5+\lambda_1-\lambda_2$, $v=3+\lambda_3-\lambda_4$:

$$\max_{\boldsymbol\lambda\ge0}\ -\tfrac1{14}\big(4u^2-2uv+2v^2\big)-(\lambda_1+\lambda_2+\lambda_3+\lambda_4).$$

Check: maximising numerically gives $\boldsymbol\lambda=(0,\,2.5,\,0,\,0)$ and dual value $-4.5$. The primal optimum is $\mathbf x^*=(-1,-0.5)$ with value $-4.5$: equal, as strong duality demands. Only $\lambda_2\gt0$, the multiplier of the active constraint $-x_1\le1$ (the other three constraints are inactive).

E7.8 · Dual of a hard-margin problem book 7.8

Derive the Lagrangian dual of $\min_{\mathbf w}\tfrac12\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w$ subject to $\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x\ge1$.

Solution

Write the constraint as $1-\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x\le0$ and introduce $\lambda\ge0$: $\mathfrak L(\mathbf w,\lambda)=\tfrac12\mathbf w^\top\mathbf w+\lambda(1-\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x)$. Minimise over $\mathbf w$: $\nabla_{\mathbf w}\mathfrak L=\mathbf w-\lambda\mathbf x=0\Rightarrow\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf x$. Substitute:

$$\mathfrak D(\lambda)=\tfrac12\lambda^2\|\mathbf x\|^2+\lambda-\lambda^2\|\mathbf x\|^2=\lambda-\tfrac12\lambda^2\|\mathbf x\|^2,\qquad\text{dual: }\max_{\lambda\ge0}\ \lambda-\tfrac12\lambda^2\|\mathbf x\|^2.$$

The maximiser is $\lambda=1/\|\mathbf x\|^2$ with value $\dfrac1{2\|\mathbf x\|^2}$, which equals the primal optimum ($\mathbf w^*=\mathbf x/\|\mathbf x\|^2$, $\tfrac12\|\mathbf w^*\|^2=\frac1{2\|\mathbf x\|^2}$) ✓. This is the one-example version of the SVM dual (Chapter 12).

E7.9 · Conjugate of the negative entropy book 7.9

Find $f^*(\mathbf s)$ for $f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{d=1}^Dx_d\log x_d$.

Solution

$f^*(\mathbf s)=\sup_{\mathbf x}\sum_d\big(s_dx_d-x_d\log x_d\big)$, separable over coordinates. For each $d$ set the derivative to zero: $s_d-\log x_d-1=0\Rightarrow x_d=e^{s_d-1}$. The value there is $s_dx_d-x_d(s_d-1)=x_d=e^{s_d-1}$. Hence

$$f^*(\mathbf s)=\sum_{d=1}^De^{s_d-1}.$$

(The negative entropy is convex for $x\gt0$, so this stationary point is the supremum.)

E7.10 · Conjugate of a quadratic book 7.10

Find the convex conjugate of $f(\mathbf x)=\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x+\mathbf b^\top\mathbf x+c$ with $A$ strictly positive definite.

Solution

Maximise $\mathbf s^\top\mathbf x-f(\mathbf x)$: the gradient $\mathbf s-A\mathbf x-\mathbf b=\mathbf 0$ gives $\mathbf x=A^{-1}(\mathbf s-\mathbf b)$. Substituting, with $\mathbf d=\mathbf s-\mathbf b$:

$$f^*(\mathbf s)=\mathbf d^\top A^{-1}\mathbf d-\tfrac12\mathbf d^\top A^{-1}\mathbf d-c=\tfrac12(\mathbf s-\mathbf b)^\top A^{-1}(\mathbf s-\mathbf b)-c.$$

E7.11 · Conjugate of the hinge loss, and its smoothing book 7.11

Hinge loss $L(\alpha)=\max\{0,1-\alpha\}$. (a) Compute $L^*(\beta)$. (b) Add a proximal term and compute the conjugate of $L^*(\beta)+\frac\gamma2\beta^2$.

Solution

(a) $L^*(\beta)=\sup_\alpha\big(\alpha\beta-\max\{0,1-\alpha\}\big)$. If $\beta\gt0$: let $\alpha\to\infty$, the value $\to+\infty$. If $\beta\lt-1$: let $\alpha\to-\infty$ where $\alpha\beta-(1-\alpha)=\alpha(\beta+1)-1\to+\infty$. For $-1\le\beta\le0$: for $\alpha\ge1$ the value is $\alpha\beta\le\beta$; for $\alpha\le1$ it is $\alpha(\beta+1)-1$, which increases in $\alpha$ and so is at most $\beta$ (at $\alpha=1$). The sup is attained at $\alpha=1$:

$$L^*(\beta)=\begin{cases}\beta&-1\le\beta\le0\\+\infty&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

(b) The conjugate of $L^*(\beta)+\frac\gamma2\beta^2$ is $\sup_{\beta\in[-1,0]}\big[(\alpha-1)\beta-\frac\gamma2\beta^2\big]$. The unconstrained maximiser is $\beta=(\alpha-1)/\gamma$; clip it to $[-1,0]$:

$$\Big(L^*+\tfrac\gamma2\beta^2\Big)^*(\alpha)=\begin{cases}0&\alpha\ge1\\\dfrac{(\alpha-1)^2}{2\gamma}&1-\gamma\le\alpha\lt1\\1-\alpha-\dfrac\gamma2&\alpha\lt1-\gamma.\end{cases}$$

This is the smoothed hinge loss: the sharp kink at $\alpha=1$ is replaced by a quadratic piece of width $\gamma$, so the function is differentiable and gradient methods such as L-BFGS apply (as the exercise wanted). As $\gamma\to0$ it recovers the hinge loss. (Matches a brute-force numeric check at several $\alpha$.)
