Chapter 5 · Vector Calculus

How does the output change when I nudge the input? The engine behind "training" a model.

Why this chapter?

Training a model means finding parameters that make a loss small. To improve the parameters you need to know which way is downhill. That direction is the gradient. Everything in this chapter is a way to compute gradients correctly and efficiently, culminating in backpropagation, the algorithm that trains neural networks.

ObjectInput → outputDerivative is…

5.1Differentiation of Univariate Functions

The derivative as a limit

The slope of a straight line is "rise over run". For a curve, take two nearby points: the difference quotient is the slope of the line through them, and the derivative is what it tends to as the gap $h$ shrinks to zero:

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Slide $h$ toward $0$ and the orange secant becomes the green tangent. The "$|x|$" preset shows a function that has no derivative at its kink: left and right slopes disagree.

Rules you need

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Sum: }&(f+g)'=f'+g' &\qquad\text{Product: }&(fg)'=f'g+fg'\\ \text{Quotient: }&\Big(\tfrac fg\Big)'=\tfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2} &\qquad\text{Chain: }&\big(g(f(x))\big)'=g'(f(x))\,f'(x)\end{aligned}$$

The chain rule is the star: derivatives of nested functions multiply. Book Example 5.5: $h(x)=(2x+1)^4$ is $g(f(x))$ with $f=2x+1$, $g=f^4$, so $h'(x)=4(2x+1)^3\cdot2=8(2x+1)^3$. Remember this, backprop is just the chain rule applied systematically.

Taylor series: approximate by a polynomial

Any smooth function looks like a polynomial near a point $x_0$, and the polynomial is built from the derivatives there. The Taylor polynomial of degree $n$:

$$T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$$

Degree 0 is a flat line at $f(x_0)$, degree 1 is the tangent line, degree 2 adds curvature, and so on. For $x_0=0$ it's called a Maclaurin series.

Book Example 5.4: for $\sin x+\cos x$ at $0$, the coefficients are $1,1,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac16,\dots$ (each sign twice). Book Example 5.3: $x^4$ at $x_0=1$ is a polynomial, so $T_4$ is exactly $x^4$ (the "x⁴" preset: the error drops to zero at degree 4).

In short. Derivative = limit of slopes. Chain rule: derivatives of nested functions multiply. Taylor: a function ≈ polynomial built from its derivatives.
Check yourselfDifferentiate $(3x^2+1)^5$.
Answer: chain rule: $5(3x^2+1)^4\cdot6x=30x(3x^2+1)^4$.

5.2Partial Differentiation and Gradients

With several inputs, change one variable at a time and freeze the rest. That's a partial derivative. Collect them all and you have the gradient:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf x}f=\frac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$$

Note the book writes the gradient as a row vector. That makes the shapes work out for the chain rule and for vector-valued functions (next section). Many other texts use a column.

ExampleBook Examples 5.6 and 5.7

$f(x,y)=(x+2y^3)^2\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2(x+2y^3),\ \ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=12(x+2y^3)y^2$ (chain rule).

$f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2x_2+x_1x_2^3\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1x_2+x_2^3&x_1^2+3x_1x_2^2\end{bmatrix}$.

What the gradient means

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is how steep that is. It is always perpendicular to the contour (level) line through the point. So $-\nabla f$ is the steepest descent direction, which is exactly what gradient descent (Ch. 7) follows. The colours show $f$ (orange high, blue low):

Chain rule for several variables stays the same shape: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf x}g(f(\mathbf x))=\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial f}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf x}$, just with matrices (order matters). Product and sum rules extend likewise.

In short. Gradient = vector of partial derivatives = direction of steepest ascent, ⟂ to contour lines. Go against it to descend.
Check yourselfGradient of $f(x,y)=x^2+3y$ at $(1,2)$?
Answer: $[2x,\ 3]=[2,\ 3]$.

5.3Gradients of Vector-Valued Functions

Now the function itself returns a vector, $\mathbf f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ (several outputs, several inputs). Stack the gradients of each output as rows. The result is the Jacobian, an $m\times n$ matrix:

$$J=\frac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}$$

The Jacobian is the best linear approximation of $\mathbf f$ near a point. Chapter 2 said matrices are linear maps. So near each point, a smooth function behaves like a matrix, and $|\det J|$ says how much it scales area. Below, the map is "polar to Cartesian". The tiny rectangle is bent by the real map (orange), but the Jacobian (blue) is a straight-edged parallelogram that matches it when the rectangle is small:

This is why $\det J$ appears in probability when you change variables (Ch. 6.7). For polar coordinates $|\det J|=r$, the familiar "$r\,dr\,d\theta$".

ExampleBook Example 5.9: $\mathbf f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x$

$f_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j\Rightarrow\partial f_i/\partial x_j=A_{ij}$, so $\dfrac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=A$. A linear map's derivative is the matrix itself. (Of course.)

ExampleBook Example 5.11: gradient of the least-squares loss

Linear model $\mathbf y=\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$. Loss $L(\mathbf e)=\|\mathbf e\|^2$, error $\mathbf e(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$. By the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=\underbrace{2\mathbf e^\top}_{\partial L/\partial\mathbf e}\ \underbrace{(-\Phi)}_{\partial\mathbf e/\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-2(\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta)^\top\Phi\ \in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$$
ML link. That last formula is the gradient of linear regression. Set it to zero and you recover the normal equations from Ch. 3. Chapter 9 does exactly this.
In short. Jacobian = matrix of all partial derivatives ($m\times n$) = local linear map. Chain rule = multiply Jacobians.
Check yourself$\mathbf f(x,y)=(xy,\ x+y)$. What is $J$?
Answer: $\begin{bmatrix}y&x\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$.

5.4Gradients of Matrices

Sometimes the thing you differentiate with respect to is a matrix, or the output is a matrix (say $f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf{x}$ with respect to the entries of $A$). The Jacobian is then a tensor: a multi-dimensional array. For $\mathbf f\in\mathbb{R}^M$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ it has shape $M\times(M\times N)$.

Two ways to cope, both from the book:

  1. Compute entry by entry: $\partial f_i/\partial A_{jk}$, then collect into the tensor. It is mechanical (and book Example 5.12 shows only the entries with $i=j$ are nonzero: $\partial f_i/\partial A_{iq}=x_q$).
  2. Vectorise: reshape each matrix into one long vector ($A\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times2}\to\mathbb{R}^8$), then use ordinary Jacobians.

Don't memorise tensor shapes. Always track dimensions: "gradient of $\mathbb{R}^a$-valued thing with respect to $\mathbb{R}^b$-valued thing is $a\times b$".

5.5Useful Identities for Computing Gradients

Memorise a handful, look up the rest (the book credits the Matrix Cookbook). With $\mathbf a$ constant:

DerivativeResult
$\dfrac{\partial\,\mathbf x^\top\mathbf a}{\partial\mathbf x}$ and $\dfrac{\partial\,\mathbf a^\top\mathbf x}{\partial\mathbf x}$$\mathbf a^\top$
$\dfrac{\partial\,\mathbf a^\top X\mathbf b}{\partial X}$$\mathbf a\mathbf b^\top$
$\dfrac{\partial\,\mathbf x^\top B\mathbf x}{\partial\mathbf x}$$\mathbf x^\top(B+B^\top)$ (equals $2\mathbf x^\top B$ if $B$ is symmetric)
$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf s}(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^\top W(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)$$-2(\mathbf x-A\mathbf s)^\top WA$ for symmetric $W$
$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial X}\det f(X)$$\det f(X)\,\operatorname{tr}\!\big(f(X)^{-1}\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}\big)$
$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial X}f(X)^{-1}$$-f(X)^{-1}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}f(X)^{-1}$
$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial X}\operatorname{tr}f(X)$$\operatorname{tr}\!\big(\tfrac{\partial f}{\partial X}\big)$

The 4th row is the least-squares gradient from 5.3, generalised with a weight matrix $W$.

5.6Backpropagation and Automatic Differentiation

Take the book's innocent-looking function

$$f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}+\cos\!\big(x^2+e^{x^2}\big)$$

Differentiating it by hand (the book's (5.110)) gives a long expression: $2x\big(\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}}-\sin(x^2+e^{x^2})\big)(1+e^{x^2})$. For a neural network with millions of parameters, writing the formula out is hopeless. Instead, break the function into small steps (a computation graph), each with a trivial local derivative:

$$a=x^2,\quad b=e^a,\quad c=a+b,\quad d=\sqrt c,\quad e=\cos c,\quad f=d+e$$
  • Forward pass: compute the values left to right (blue).
  • Backward pass: start with $\partial f/\partial f=1$ and move right to left. At each node, multiply by the local derivative, and where two paths merge, add (that is the chain rule). This is backpropagation.
Try it.

Notice the cost: the backward pass has about the same number of operations as the forward pass, no matter how ugly the final formula is. That is what makes training deep networks feasible.

In a deep network

A network is a long chain $\mathbf f_K\circ\dots\circ\mathbf f_1(\mathbf x)$ with each layer $\mathbf f_i=\sigma(A_{i-1}\mathbf f_{i-1}+\mathbf b_{i-1})$. To get $\partial L/\partial\theta_i$ for layer $i$, the chain rule multiplies Jacobians from the output down to layer $i$. Computing layer $i$ reuses everything already computed for layer $i+1$, so one backward sweep gives all the gradients.

Reverse vs forward mode

Because $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{db}\frac{db}{da}\frac{da}{dx}$ is a product, you can multiply from the right end (forward mode) or from the left end (reverse mode = backprop). Reverse mode is far cheaper when there are many inputs (parameters) and one output (the loss), which is the neural-network case. In general this technique is automatic differentiation: exact (up to rounding), unlike finite differences, and not symbolic like algebra.

In short. Break a function into small steps, then propagate derivatives backward: multiply along paths, add at joins. Cost ≈ cost of the forward pass.
Check yourselfFor $f=(x+1)^2$ split as $a=x+1,\ f=a^2$, what are $\partial f/\partial a$ and $\partial f/\partial x$ at $x=2$?
Answer: $a=3$, $\partial f/\partial a=2a=6$, and $\partial a/\partial x=1$, so $\partial f/\partial x=6$.

5.7Higher-Order Derivatives

The derivative of the derivative tells you about curvature. For $f(x,y)$ the second derivatives collect into the Hessian:

$$H=\nabla^2f=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\partial y}\\[2mm]\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}\end{bmatrix}$$

For nice functions it is symmetric (mixed partials agree). Its eigenvalues (Ch. 4!) are the curvatures in the principal directions: all positive → a bowl (a minimum candidate), all negative → a dome, mixed signs → a saddle. The readout of the gradient widget in 5.2 shows the Hessian and its eigenvalues at the chosen point: try the saddle.

ML link. Second-order optimisation methods (Newton's method) use the Hessian to take smarter steps than plain gradient descent.

5.8Linearization and Multivariate Taylor Series

The 1D Taylor idea extends. Near a point $\mathbf x_0$, with $\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0$:

$$f(\mathbf x)\approx\underbrace{f(\mathbf x_0)}_{\text{height}}+\underbrace{\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\,\boldsymbol\delta}_{\text{tilted plane}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H(\mathbf x_0)\,\boldsymbol\delta}_{\text{curvature}}+\cdots$$

Keeping the first two terms is linearization: replace the function by its tangent plane. Add the third and you capture curvature with a quadratic. These approximations are the reason local methods work: zoom in far enough and everything looks like a plane or a bowl.

In short. $f(\mathbf x)\approx f(\mathbf x_0)+\nabla f\,\boldsymbol\delta+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H\boldsymbol\delta$. Gradient = slope of the tangent plane, Hessian = curvature.
Check yourself$f(x)=x^2$ at $x_0=1$: what is the linear approximation?
Answer: $f(1)+f'(1)(x-1)=1+2(x-1)$, the tangent line.
