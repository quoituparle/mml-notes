How does the output change when I nudge the input? The engine behind "training" a model.

Why this chapter?

Training a model means finding parameters that make a loss small. To improve the parameters you need to know which way is downhill. That direction is the gradient. Everything in this chapter is a way to compute gradients correctly and efficiently, culminating in backpropagation, the algorithm that trains neural networks.

5.1Differentiation of Univariate Functions

The derivative as a limit

The slope of a straight line is "rise over run". For a curve, take two nearby points: the difference quotient is the slope of the line through them, and the derivative is what it tends to as the gap $h$ shrinks to zero:

$$f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$$

Slide $h$ toward $0$ and the orange secant becomes the green tangent. The "$|x|$" preset shows a function that has no derivative at its kink: left and right slopes disagree.

Rules you need

$$\begin{aligned}\text{Sum: }&(f+g)'=f'+g' &\qquad\text{Product: }&(fg)'=f'g+fg'\\ \text{Quotient: }&\Big(\tfrac fg\Big)'=\tfrac{f'g-fg'}{g^2} &\qquad\text{Chain: }&\big(g(f(x))\big)'=g'(f(x))\,f'(x)\end{aligned}$$

The chain rule is the star: derivatives of nested functions multiply. Book Example 5.5: $h(x)=(2x+1)^4$ is $g(f(x))$ with $f=2x+1$, $g=f^4$, so $h'(x)=4(2x+1)^3\cdot2=8(2x+1)^3$. Remember this, backprop is just the chain rule applied systematically.

Taylor series: approximate by a polynomial

Any smooth function looks like a polynomial near a point $x_0$, and the polynomial is built from the derivatives there. The Taylor polynomial of degree $n$:

$$T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$$

Degree 0 is a flat line at $f(x_0)$, degree 1 is the tangent line, degree 2 adds curvature, and so on. For $x_0=0$ it's called a Maclaurin series.

Book Example 5.4: for $\sin x+\cos x$ at $0$, the coefficients are $1,1,-\tfrac12,-\tfrac16,\dots$ (each sign twice). Book Example 5.3: $x^4$ at $x_0=1$ is a polynomial, so $T_4$ is exactly $x^4$ (the "x⁴" preset: the error drops to zero at degree 4).

Going deeper: deriving a derivative, a derivative table, and Taylor in detail

Book Ex. 5.2 Why $\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}$ (from the definition)

Expand $(x+h)^n=\sum_{i=0}^n\binom nix^{n-i}h^i$. The $i=0$ term is $x^n$ and cancels with $-x^n$, so

$$\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}=\sum_{i=1}^n\binom nix^{n-i}h^{i-1}=nx^{n-1}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=2}^n\binom nix^{n-i}h^{i-1}}_{\to0\text{ as }h\to0}\ \longrightarrow\ nx^{n-1}.$$

$e^x$$e^x$$\cos x$$-\sin x$

Differentiable implies continuous, but not the other way around: $|x|$ is continuous at 0 with no derivative there (the slopes from left and right disagree). The book assumes differentiability throughout and notes that sub-gradients extend most ideas to such kinks.

Taylor series: the details

  • The coefficient of $(x-x_0)^k$ is $f^{(k)}(x_0)/k!$; the factorial makes the $k$th derivative of the polynomial match $f^{(k)}(x_0)$ exactly. So $T_n$ agrees with $f$ in value and its first $n$ derivatives at $x_0$.
  • If $f$ is itself a polynomial of degree $\le n$, then $T_n=f$ exactly. Book Ex. 5.3: $f(x)=x^4$ at $x_0=1$ has $f(1)=1,f'(1)=4,f''(1)=12,f^{(3)}(1)=24,f^{(4)}(1)=24$ and $T_6(x)=1+4(x-1)+6(x-1)^2+4(x-1)^3+(x-1)^4=x^4$.
  • Taylor series: $T_\infty(x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{f^{(k)}(x_0)}{k!}(x-x_0)^k$ for smooth ($C^\infty$) $f$; at $x_0=0$ it is called the Maclaurin series. If $T_\infty=f$ the function is analytic.
  • Book Ex. 5.4 splits cleanly: $\sin x+\cos x=\sum_k(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k}}{(2k)!}+\sum_k(-1)^k\frac{x^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}$, the cosine series plus the sine series.
  • Caveat: the approximation is good only near $x_0$ (and some series converge only on a limited interval). Higher degree widens the good region, as the widget shows.
In short. Derivative = limit of slopes. Chain rule: derivatives of nested functions multiply. Taylor: a function ≈ polynomial built from its derivatives.

5.2Partial Differentiation and Gradients

With several inputs, change one variable at a time and freeze the rest. That's a partial derivative. Collect them all and you have the gradient:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf x}f=\frac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$$

Note the book writes the gradient as a row vector. That makes the shapes work out for the chain rule and for vector-valued functions (next section). Many other texts use a column.

ExampleBook Examples 5.6 and 5.7

$f(x,y)=(x+2y^3)^2\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}=2(x+2y^3),\ \ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}=12(x+2y^3)y^2$ (chain rule).

$f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2x_2+x_1x_2^3\Rightarrow\ \dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}2x_1x_2+x_2^3&x_1^2+3x_1x_2^2\end{bmatrix}$.

What the gradient means

The gradient points in the direction of steepest ascent, and its length is how steep that is. It is always perpendicular to the contour (level) line through the point. So $-\nabla f$ is the steepest descent direction, which is exactly what gradient descent (Ch. 7) follows. The colours show $f$ (orange high, blue low):

Chain rule for several variables stays the same shape: $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf x}g(f(\mathbf x))=\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial f}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial\mathbf x}$, just with matrices (order matters). Product and sum rules extend likewise.

Going deeper: partial derivatives and the multivariate chain rule

Definition. $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x_1,\dots,x_i+h,\dots,x_n)-f(\mathbf x)}{h}$: nudge one input, hold the others fixed. All the usual product, sum and chain rules apply (just mind the order when matrices appear).

Chain rule with several paths

If $f(x_1,x_2)$ and each $x_i$ depends on $t$, then $\dfrac{df}{dt}=\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\dfrac{dx_1}{dt}+\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_2}\dfrac{dx_2}{dt}$: sum over all paths from $t$ to $f$. In matrix form this is the product (row gradient)$\times$(column of derivatives), and if the $x_i$ depend on two variables $s,t$ you get a row times a $2\times2$ matrix.

Worked example

$f(x_1,x_2)=x_1^2+2x_2$ with $x_1=\sin t$, $x_2=\cos t$. Then $\partial f/\partial x_1=2x_1$, $\partial f/\partial x_2=2$, so $\dfrac{df}{dt}=2\sin t\cos t+2(-\sin t)=2\sin t\cos t-2\sin t$. (Direct check: $f=\sin^2t+2\cos t$ gives the same.)

Which way is uphill? The directional derivative

The rate of change of $f$ in the unit direction $\mathbf u$ is $\nabla f\,\mathbf u=\|\nabla f\|\cos\omega$, where $\omega$ is the angle between $\mathbf u$ and the gradient (Chapter 3). It is largest ($\|\nabla f\|$) when $\mathbf u$ points along the gradient, zero when $\mathbf u$ is perpendicular to it (along a contour line), and most negative against it. That is the precise statement behind "gradient = steepest ascent".

In short. Gradient = vector of partial derivatives = direction of steepest ascent, ⟂ to contour lines. Go against it to descend.

5.3Gradients of Vector-Valued Functions

Now the function itself returns a vector, $\mathbf f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ (several outputs, several inputs). Stack the gradients of each output as rows. The result is the Jacobian, an $m\times n$ matrix:

$$J=\frac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n}\\ \vdots&&\vdots\\ \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1}&\cdots&\dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}\end{bmatrix}$$

The Jacobian is the best linear approximation of $\mathbf f$ near a point. Chapter 2 said matrices are linear maps. So near each point, a smooth function behaves like a matrix, and $|\det J|$ says how much it scales area. Below, the map is "polar to Cartesian". The tiny rectangle is bent by the real map (orange), but the Jacobian (blue) is a straight-edged parallelogram that matches it when the rectangle is small:

This is why $\det J$ appears in probability when you change variables (Ch. 6.7). For polar coordinates $|\det J|=r$, the familiar "$r\,dr\,d\theta$".

ExampleBook Example 5.9: $\mathbf f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf x$

$f_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j\Rightarrow\partial f_i/\partial x_j=A_{ij}$, so $\dfrac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=A$. A linear map's derivative is the matrix itself. (Of course.)

ExampleBook Example 5.11: gradient of the least-squares loss

Linear model $\mathbf y=\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$. Loss $L(\mathbf e)=\|\mathbf e\|^2$, error $\mathbf e(\boldsymbol\theta)=\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$. By the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=\underbrace{2\mathbf e^\top}_{\partial L/\partial\mathbf e}\ \underbrace{(-\Phi)}_{\partial\mathbf e/\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-2(\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta)^\top\Phi\ \in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$$
ML link. That last formula is the gradient of linear regression. Set it to zero and you recover the normal equations from Ch. 3. Chapter 9 does exactly this.

Going deeper: shapes, the chain rule as matrix multiplication, and worked examples

Shape rule. For $\mathbf f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ the Jacobian is $m\times n$: row $i$ is the gradient of output $f_i$. A scalar function is the case $m=1$ (a row). Chain rule for $\mathbb{R}^n\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf g\ }\mathbb{R}^k\xrightarrow{\ \mathbf f\ }\mathbb{R}^m$: $\dfrac{d}{d\mathbf x}\mathbf f(\mathbf g(\mathbf x))=\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf g}}_{m\times k}\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial\mathbf g}{\partial\mathbf x}}_{k\times n}$, and the inner dimensions must match, which is a handy check.

Book Ex. 5.10 Chain rule through a vector function

$f(\mathbf x)=\exp(x_1x_2^2)$ and $\mathbf x=\mathbf g(t)=(t\cos t,\ t\sin t)$, $h=f\circ\mathbf g$. Shapes: $\partial f/\partial\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times2}$, $\partial\mathbf g/\partial t\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times1}$. Then

$$\frac{dh}{dt}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{x_1x_2^2}x_2^2&2e^{x_1x_2^2}x_1x_2\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\cos t-t\sin t\\\sin t+t\cos t\end{bmatrix}=e^{x_1x_2^2}\big(x_2^2(\cos t-t\sin t)+2x_1x_2(\sin t+t\cos t)\big)$$

with $x_1=t\cos t$, $x_2=t\sin t$ substituted.

Book Ex. 5.11 Gradient of the least-squares loss, step by step

Model $\mathbf y=\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$ with $\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$ (features), $\boldsymbol\theta\in\mathbb{R}^D$ (parameters). Loss $L=\|\mathbf e\|^2$, error $\mathbf e=\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta$. Shapes: $\partial L/\partial\mathbf e=2\mathbf e^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}$, $\partial\mathbf e/\partial\boldsymbol\theta=-\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D}$, so $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol\theta}=-2\mathbf e^\top\Phi=-2(\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta)^\top\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$. Setting it to zero gives $\Phi^\top\Phi\boldsymbol\theta=\Phi^\top\mathbf y$: the normal equations (Chapter 3.8) and the closed-form linear regression solution of Chapter 9. Expanding $L=(\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta)^\top(\mathbf y-\Phi\boldsymbol\theta)$ first and differentiating gives the same answer without the chain rule, but that doesn't scale to deep compositions.

The Jacobian also tells you how a map stretches space locally: $|\det J|$ for square $J$ is the local area/volume scale factor (the polar-coordinates widget, $|\det J|=r$). This is exactly the factor needed to change variables in probability (Chapter 6.7).

In short. Jacobian = matrix of all partial derivatives ($m\times n$) = local linear map. Chain rule = multiply Jacobians.

5.4Gradients of Matrices

Sometimes the thing you differentiate with respect to is a matrix, or the output is a matrix (say $f(\mathbf x)=A\mathbf{x}$ with respect to the entries of $A$). The Jacobian is then a tensor: a multi-dimensional array. For $\mathbf f\in\mathbb{R}^M$ and $A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$ it has shape $M\times(M\times N)$.

Two ways to cope, both from the book:

  1. Compute entry by entry: $\partial f_i/\partial A_{jk}$, then collect into the tensor. It is mechanical (and book Example 5.12 shows only the entries with $i=j$ are nonzero: $\partial f_i/\partial A_{iq}=x_q$).
  2. Vectorise: reshape each matrix into one long vector ($A\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times2}\to\mathbb{R}^8$), then use ordinary Jacobians.

Going deeper: the two approaches, with the book's examples

The gradient of a matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ with respect to a matrix $B\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times q}$ is a four-dimensional array with entries $J_{ijkl}=\partial A_{ij}/\partial B_{kl}$. Two equivalent ways to handle it:

  1. Tensor approach: compute each partial derivative $\partial A/\partial B_{kl}$ (an $m\times n$ matrix) and stack them.
  2. Flatten approach: reshape each matrix into a long vector (stack the columns), $A\to\tilde A\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}$, $B\to\tilde B\in\mathbb{R}^{pq}$. Then the Jacobian is an ordinary $mn\times pq$ matrix and the chain rule is plain matrix multiplication. This is usually easier in practice.

Book Ex. 5.12 Gradient of $\mathbf f=A\mathbf x$ with respect to $A$

$\mathbf f\in\mathbb{R}^M$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, so $d\mathbf f/dA\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(M\times N)}$. Write $f_i=\sum_jA_{ij}x_j$; then $\partial f_i/\partial A_{iq}=x_q$ and $\partial f_i/\partial A_{kq}=0$ for $k\ne i$. So $\partial f_i/\partial A$ is a "matrix" that holds $\mathbf x^\top$ in row $i$ and zeros in every other row.

Book Ex. 5.13 Gradient of $K=R^\top R$ with respect to $R$

$R\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, $K\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}$, so $dK/dR\in\mathbb{R}^{(N\times N)\times(M\times N)}$. Entry $K_{pq}=\sum_mR_{mp}R_{mq}$ (dot product of columns $p$ and $q$). Differentiating with respect to $R_{ij}$: $\partial K_{pq}/\partial R_{ij}=R_{iq}$ if $j=p\ne q$; $=R_{ip}$ if $j=q\ne p$; $=2R_{iq}$ if $j=p=q$; and $0$ otherwise.

Don't memorise tensor shapes. Always track dimensions: "gradient of $\mathbb{R}^a$-valued thing with respect to $\mathbb{R}^b$-valued thing is $a\times b$".

Going deeper: using the identities, with checks

Quadratic form

$f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^\top B\mathbf x$ with $B=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{bmatrix}$. Identity: $\nabla f=\mathbf x^\top(B+B^\top)=2\mathbf x^\top B=(4x_1+2x_2,\ 2x_1+6x_2)$. Direct check: $f=2x_1^2+2x_1x_2+3x_2^2$, so $\partial f/\partial x_1=4x_1+2x_2$ ✓ and $\partial f/\partial x_2=2x_1+6x_2$ ✓.

Bilinear form

$g(X)=\mathbf a^\top X\mathbf b$ with $X\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times F}$: $\partial g/\partial X_{ij}=a_ib_j$, i.e. the outer product $\mathbf a\mathbf b^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times F}$ (same shape as $X$).

Two more used all the time in statistics (consequences of the determinant/inverse identities): for $f(X)=X$, $\partial\det X/\partial X=\det(X)\,X^{-\top}$, hence $\partial\log\det X/\partial X=X^{-\top}$ (it appears in every Gaussian log-likelihood); and $\partial(X^{-1})=-X^{-1}(\partial X)X^{-1}$ (the matrix version of $\frac{d}{dx}\frac1x=-\frac1{x^2}$).

Always sanity-check a matrix gradient by (1) confirming the shape and (2) comparing with a finite difference $\frac{f(\mathbf x+h\mathbf e_i)-f(\mathbf x-h\mathbf e_i)}{2h}$ on a small random example. This is exactly how deep-learning libraries test their own derivatives.

The 4th row is the least-squares gradient from 5.3, generalised with a weight matrix $W$.

5.6Backpropagation and Automatic Differentiation

Take the book's innocent-looking function

$$f(x)=\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}+\cos\!\big(x^2+e^{x^2}\big)$$

Differentiating it by hand (the book's (5.110)) gives a long expression: $2x\big(\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x^2+e^{x^2}}}-\sin(x^2+e^{x^2})\big)(1+e^{x^2})$. For a neural network with millions of parameters, writing the formula out is hopeless. Instead, break the function into small steps (a computation graph), each with a trivial local derivative:

$$a=x^2,\quad b=e^a,\quad c=a+b,\quad d=\sqrt c,\quad e=\cos c,\quad f=d+e$$
  • Forward pass: compute the values left to right (blue).
  • Backward pass: start with $\partial f/\partial f=1$ and move right to left. At each node, multiply by the local derivative, and where two paths merge, add (that is the chain rule). This is backpropagation.
Try it.

Notice the cost: the backward pass has about the same number of operations as the forward pass, no matter how ugly the final formula is. That is what makes training deep networks feasible.

In a deep network

A network is a long chain $\mathbf f_K\circ\dots\circ\mathbf f_1(\mathbf x)$ with each layer $\mathbf f_i=\sigma(A_{i-1}\mathbf f_{i-1}+\mathbf b_{i-1})$. To get $\partial L/\partial\theta_i$ for layer $i$, the chain rule multiplies Jacobians from the output down to layer $i$. Computing layer $i$ reuses everything already computed for layer $i+1$, so one backward sweep gives all the gradients.

Reverse vs forward mode

Because $\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy}{db}\frac{db}{da}\frac{da}{dx}$ is a product, you can multiply from the right end (forward mode) or from the left end (reverse mode = backprop). Reverse mode is far cheaper when there are many inputs (parameters) and one output (the loss), which is the neural-network case. In general this technique is automatic differentiation: exact (up to rounding), unlike finite differences, and not symbolic like algebra.

Going deeper: backprop in a deep network, a numeric example, and the general algorithm

Deep networks

With $\mathbf f_0=\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf f_i=\sigma_i(A_{i-1}\mathbf f_{i-1}+\mathbf b_{i-1})$ for layers $i=1..K$, and loss $L=\|\mathbf y-\mathbf f_K\|^2$, the chain rule gives, for the parameters $\theta_{i}=\{A_i,\mathbf b_i\}$ of layer $i+1$,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial\theta_i}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf f_K}\frac{\partial\mathbf f_K}{\partial\mathbf f_{K-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial\mathbf f_{i+2}}{\partial\mathbf f_{i+1}}\ \frac{\partial\mathbf f_{i+1}}{\partial\theta_i}.$$

The first factors (output of a layer with respect to its input) are shared by every earlier layer, so they are computed once while sweeping backward: keep a running row $\boldsymbol\delta_i=\partial L/\partial\mathbf f_i$ and update $\boldsymbol\delta_{i}=\boldsymbol\delta_{i+1}\,\partial\mathbf f_{i+1}/\partial\mathbf f_i$. The price: the forward pass must store its intermediate values for use on the way back.

A tiny numeric example

One neuron: $z=wx+b$, $\hat y=\sigma(z)$, $L=(y-\hat y)^2$ with $x=2$, $y=1$, $w=0.5$, $b=-1$. Forward: $z=0$, $\hat y=0.5$, $L=0.25$. Backward: $\partial L/\partial\hat y=-2(y-\hat y)=-1$; $\partial\hat y/\partial z=\hat y(1-\hat y)=0.25$; $\partial z/\partial w=x=2$, $\partial z/\partial b=1$. Multiply along the path: $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}=(-1)(0.25)(2)=-0.5$ and $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}=(-1)(0.25)(1)=-0.25$. Both negative: increasing $w$ or $b$ would reduce the loss, so gradient descent will raise them.

The general algorithm

Write the computation as a graph: inputs $x_1..x_d$, intermediate variables $x_{d+1},\dots,x_{D-1}$, output $x_D$, each defined by an elementary function of its parent nodes: $x_i=g_i(x_{\mathrm{Pa}(x_i)})$ (forward propagation). Set $\partial f/\partial x_D=1$ and sweep backward: $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{j:\,x_i\in\mathrm{Pa}(x_j)}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}\dfrac{\partial g_j}{\partial x_i}$ (back-propagation; the sum collects all paths).

  • Forward vs reverse mode: for a function with $n$ inputs and $m$ outputs, forward mode needs about $n$ sweeps, reverse mode about $m$. A training loss has one output and millions of inputs (parameters), so reverse mode (backprop) wins.
  • Automatic differentiation is exact up to rounding, unlike finite differences (which are approximate) and unlike symbolic differentiation (which can produce huge expressions). It works on computer programs, but loops and if-statements need care, and every elementary operation must be differentiable.
In short. Break a function into small steps, then propagate derivatives backward: multiply along paths, add at joins. Cost ≈ cost of the forward pass.

5.7Higher-Order Derivatives

The derivative of the derivative tells you about curvature. For $f(x,y)$ the second derivatives collect into the Hessian:

$$H=\nabla^2f=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\partial y}\\[2mm]\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\partial x}&\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2}\end{bmatrix}$$

For nice functions it is symmetric (mixed partials agree). Its eigenvalues (Ch. 4!) are the curvatures in the principal directions: all positive → a bowl (a minimum candidate), all negative → a dome, mixed signs → a saddle. The readout of the gradient widget in 5.2 shows the Hessian and its eigenvalues at the chosen point: try the saddle.

Going deeper: notation and symmetry

Second partial derivatives: $\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}$ (twice in $x$), $\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\,\partial x}=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Big)$ (first $x$, then $y$), and so on. If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable the order doesn't matter, $\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\partial y}=\dfrac{\partial^2f}{\partial y\partial x}$, so the Hessian is symmetric. For $f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$ it is $n\times n$ and measures curvature: curvature along a unit direction $\mathbf u$ is $\mathbf u^\top H\mathbf u$. For a vector field $\mathbf f:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m$ the "Hessian" is an $m\times n\times n$ tensor. Example (book Ex. 5.15): $f=x^2+2xy+y^3$ has $H=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&6y\end{bmatrix}$, so $H(1,2)=\begin{bmatrix}2&2\\2&12\end{bmatrix}$ (positive definite: a bowl there).

ML link. Second-order optimisation methods (Newton's method) use the Hessian to take smarter steps than plain gradient descent.

5.8Linearization and Multivariate Taylor Series

The 1D Taylor idea extends. Near a point $\mathbf x_0$, with $\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0$:

$$f(\mathbf x)\approx\underbrace{f(\mathbf x_0)}_{\text{height}}+\underbrace{\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\,\boldsymbol\delta}_{\text{tilted plane}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H(\mathbf x_0)\,\boldsymbol\delta}_{\text{curvature}}+\cdots$$

Keeping the first two terms is linearization: replace the function by its tangent plane. Add the third and you capture curvature with a quadratic. These approximations are the reason local methods work: zoom in far enough and everything looks like a plane or a bowl.

Going deeper: the full multivariate Taylor series, with a worked example

For $f:\mathbb{R}^D\to\mathbb{R}$ smooth at $\mathbf x_0$ and $\boldsymbol\delta=\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0$, $f(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty\dfrac{D^k_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x_0)}{k!}\boldsymbol\delta^k$, where $D^kf$ is the $k$th-order derivative tensor and $\boldsymbol\delta^k=\boldsymbol\delta\otimes\cdots\otimes\boldsymbol\delta$ is a $k$-fold outer product. The first terms:

$k$TermWhat it is
0$f(\mathbf x_0)$constant (height)
1$\nabla f(\mathbf x_0)\,\boldsymbol\delta$linear (tilt), $\sum_i\partial_if\,\delta_i$
2$\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H(\mathbf x_0)\boldsymbol\delta$quadratic (curvature), $\tfrac12\sum_{ij}H_{ij}\delta_i\delta_j$
3$\tfrac1{3!}\sum_{ijk}D^3f[i,j,k]\delta_i\delta_j\delta_k$cubic

Book Ex. 5.15 Taylor expansion of a two-variable polynomial

$f(x,y)=x^2+2xy+y^3$ at $(x_0,y_0)=(1,2)$. A cubic polynomial should be reproduced exactly by terms up to order 3.

  • $k=0$: $f(1,2)=1+4+8=13$.
  • $k=1$: $\nabla f=[2x+2y,\ 2x+3y^2]$, so $\nabla f(1,2)=[6,\ 14]$. Term: $6(x-1)+14(y-2)$.
  • $k=2$: $H(1,2)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&2\\2&12\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Term: $\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H\boldsymbol\delta=(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-2)+6(y-2)^2$.
  • $k=3$: the only nonzero third derivative is $\partial^3f/\partial y^3=6$. Term: $\tfrac16\cdot6\,(y-2)^3=(y-2)^3$.
$$f(x,y)=13+6(x-1)+14(y-2)+(x-1)^2+2(x-1)(y-2)+6(y-2)^2+(y-2)^3.$$

Expanding the right-hand side returns $x^2+2xy+y^3$ exactly (verified).

Why ML cares: if $\mathbf x$ is Gaussian and $f$ is nonlinear, a first-order Taylor expansion around the mean linearises $f$, and the output is then approximately Gaussian with mean $f(\boldsymbol\mu)$ and covariance $J\Sigma J^\top$ (Chapter 6). That idea powers the extended Kalman filter. The Laplace approximation uses a second-order expansion (needs the Hessian) to approximate a distribution by a Gaussian around its mode.

In short. $f(\mathbf x)\approx f(\mathbf x_0)+\nabla f\,\boldsymbol\delta+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\delta^\top H\boldsymbol\delta$. Gradient = slope of the tangent plane, Hessian = curvature.
Check yourself$f(x)=x^2$ at $x_0=1$: what is the linear approximation?
Answer: $f(1)+f'(1)(x-1)=1+2(x-1)$, the tangent line.

Exercises with solutions

All nine exercises from the book's Chapter 5 (numbers refer to the book). Every answer was checked symbolically or numerically (finite differences) by computer. Track the shape of each derivative: it's the best error detector.

E5.1 · Product and chain rule book 5.1

Differentiate $f(x)=\log(x^4)\sin(x^3)$.

Solution

For $x\gt0$, $\log(x^4)=4\log x$ with derivative $4/x$. By the product rule and the chain rule ($\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^3)=3x^2\cos(x^3)$):

$$f'(x)=\frac4x\sin(x^3)+4\log x\cdot3x^2\cos(x^3)=\frac{4\sin(x^3)}{x}+12x^2\log x\cos(x^3).$$

E5.2 · Derivative of the logistic sigmoid book 5.2

Differentiate $f(x)=\dfrac1{1+\exp(-x)}$.

Solution

Write $f=(1+e^{-x})^{-1}$. Chain rule: $f'=-(1+e^{-x})^{-2}\cdot(-e^{-x})=\dfrac{e^{-x}}{(1+e^{-x})^2}$. Nicer form: since $1-f=\dfrac{e^{-x}}{1+e^{-x}}$, we get

$$f'(x)=f(x)\,\big(1-f(x)\big).$$

The derivative is cheap to compute from the output itself: that's why the sigmoid was popular in early neural networks. It peaks at $0.25$ (at $x=0$) and vanishes for large $|x|$ (the "vanishing gradient" problem).

E5.3 · Derivative of a Gaussian shape book 5.3

Differentiate $f(x)=\exp\!\big(-\frac1{2\sigma^2}(x-\mu)^2\big)$ ($\mu,\sigma$ constants).

Solution

Chain rule with inner function $u=-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$, $u'=-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2}$:

$$f'(x)=-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big)=-\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma^2}f(x).$$

Zero at $x=\mu$ (the peak), positive to the left of the peak, negative to the right ✓.

E5.4 · Taylor polynomials book 5.4

Find $T_n$, $n=0,\dots,5$, of $f(x)=\sin x+\cos x$ at $x_0=0$.

Solution

Derivatives at 0: $f(0)=1$, $f'(0)=\cos0-\sin0=1$, $f''(0)=-1$, $f^{(3)}(0)=-1$, $f^{(4)}(0)=1$, $f^{(5)}(0)=1$ (the pattern $1,1,-1,-1$ repeats). Coefficients $f^{(k)}(0)/k!$: $1,\ 1,\ -\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac16,\ \tfrac1{24},\ \tfrac1{120}$. So

$$\begin{aligned}T_0&=1,\quad T_1=1+x,\quad T_2=1+x-\tfrac12x^2,\quad T_3=T_2-\tfrac16x^3,\\ T_4&=T_3+\tfrac1{24}x^4,\quad T_5=T_4+\tfrac1{120}x^5.\end{aligned}$$

Try these in the Taylor widget (preset "sin x + cos x").

E5.5 · Shapes and Jacobians book 5.5

(a) State the dimension of $\partial f_i/\partial\mathbf x$ and (b) compute it, for $f_1(\mathbf x)=\sin(x_1)\cos(x_2)$ ($\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^2$), $f_2(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y$ ($\mathbf x,\mathbf y\in\mathbb{R}^n$), $f_3(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top$ ($\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^n$).

Solution
  • $f_1:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}$: Jacobian $\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times2}$, $\ \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial\mathbf x}=\big[\cos x_1\cos x_2,\ \ -\sin x_1\sin x_2\big]$.
  • $f_2:\mathbb{R}^n\times\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}$: $\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial\mathbf x}=\mathbf y^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$ and $\dfrac{\partial f_2}{\partial\mathbf y}=\mathbf x^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times n}$; with respect to the stacked $(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ it is $[\mathbf y^\top\ \mathbf x^\top]\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times2n}$.
  • $f_3:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$: the Jacobian is a tensor in $\mathbb{R}^{(n\times n)\times n}$ with entries $\dfrac{\partial(x_ix_j)}{\partial x_k}=\delta_{ik}x_j+x_i\delta_{jk}$ ($\delta$ = 1 if the indices agree, else 0). Flattened, it is an $n^2\times n$ matrix.

E5.6 · A vector and a trace book 5.6

Differentiate $f(\mathbf t)=\sin(\log(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t))$ with respect to $\mathbf t\in\mathbb{R}^D$, and $g(X)=\operatorname{tr}(AXB)$ with respect to $X$ ($A\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times E}$, $X\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times F}$, $B\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times D}$).

Solution

$f$: chain rule with $z=\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t$, $\dfrac{dz}{d\mathbf t}=2\mathbf t^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$:

$$\frac{df}{d\mathbf t}=\cos\!\big(\log(\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t)\big)\cdot\frac1{\mathbf t^\top\mathbf t}\cdot2\mathbf t^\top\ \in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}.$$

$g$: write it out: $g=\sum_{i,j,k}A_{ij}X_{jk}B_{ki}$. Then $\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial X_{jk}}=\sum_iB_{ki}A_{ij}=(BA)_{kj}$. Collect over $j,k$: the gradient (same shape as $X$) is $(BA)^\top=\mathbf{A^\top B^\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times F}$. (Using the cyclic property, $g=\operatorname{tr}(BAX)$ and $\partial\operatorname{tr}(CX)/\partial X=C^\top$ with $C=BA$.) Verified numerically against finite differences.

E5.7 · Chain rule with dimensions book 5.7

(a) $f(z)=\log(1+z)$, $z=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x$, $\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D$. (b) $f(\mathbf z)=\sin(\mathbf z)$ (elementwise), $\mathbf z=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times D}$.

Solution

(a) $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial z}=\dfrac1{1+z}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial\mathbf x}=2\mathbf x^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$, so $\dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}=\dfrac{2\mathbf x^\top}{1+\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$.

(b) $\mathbf f\in\mathbb{R}^E$. $\dfrac{\partial\mathbf f}{\partial\mathbf z}=\operatorname{diag}(\cos z_1,\dots,\cos z_E)\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times E}$ (elementwise function, so only the diagonal is nonzero) and $\dfrac{\partial\mathbf z}{\partial\mathbf x}=A\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times D}$. Therefore

$$\frac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=\operatorname{diag}\big(\cos(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\big)\,A\in\mathbb{R}^{E\times D}.$$

In code you never build the diagonal matrix: multiply each row $i$ of $A$ by $\cos z_i$.

E5.8 · Three more chain rules book 5.8

(a) $f=\exp(-\frac12z)$, $z=\mathbf y^\top S^{-1}\mathbf y$, $\mathbf y=\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu$ ($\mathbf x,\boldsymbol\mu\in\mathbb{R}^D$, $S\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}$). (b) $f(\mathbf x)=\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top+\sigma^2I)$. (c) $\mathbf f=\tanh(\mathbf z)\in\mathbb{R}^M$, $\mathbf z=A\mathbf x+\mathbf b$, $A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$.

Solution

(a) Shapes: $\partial f/\partial z=-\tfrac12e^{-z/2}\in\mathbb{R}$; $\partial z/\partial\mathbf y=\mathbf y^\top(S^{-1}+S^{-\top})\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$; $\partial\mathbf y/\partial\mathbf x=I_D$. For symmetric $S$ (a covariance) this gives

$$\frac{df}{d\mathbf x}=-\exp\!\big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top S^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\big)\,(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top S^{-1}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}.$$

(This is the derivative of the unnormalised Gaussian bump from E5.3, now in $D$ dimensions.)

(b) Write out the outer product: $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf x\mathbf x^\top)=\sum_ix_i^2=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x$, and $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma^2I)=D\sigma^2$ is constant. So $f=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x+D\sigma^2$ and $\dfrac{df}{d\mathbf x}=2\mathbf x^\top\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}$.

(c) $\partial\mathbf f/\partial\mathbf z=\operatorname{diag}\big(1-\tanh^2(z_i)\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}$, $\partial\mathbf z/\partial\mathbf x=A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$, so $\dfrac{d\mathbf f}{d\mathbf x}=\operatorname{diag}\big(1-\tanh^2(A\mathbf x+\mathbf b)\big)A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}$.

E5.9 · The reparametrisation gradient book 5.9

Let $g(\mathbf x,\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)=\log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)-\log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)$ with $\mathbf z=\mathbf t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)$ ($\mathbf x\in\mathbb{R}^D,\mathbf z\in\mathbb{R}^E,\boldsymbol\nu\in\mathbb{R}^F,\boldsymbol\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^G$). Compute $\dfrac{d}{d\boldsymbol\nu}g$.

Solution

$\boldsymbol\nu$ affects $g$ in two ways: through $\mathbf z=\mathbf t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)$ and directly through $q$. The multivariate chain rule adds both paths:

$$\frac{dg}{d\boldsymbol\nu}=\underbrace{\Big(\frac{\partial\log p(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)}{\partial\mathbf z}-\frac{\partial\log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\mathbf z}\Big)}_{1\times E}\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathbf t(\boldsymbol\epsilon,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}}_{E\times F}-\underbrace{\frac{\partial\log q(\mathbf z,\boldsymbol\nu)}{\partial\boldsymbol\nu}}_{1\times F}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times F}.$$

This is the "reparametrisation trick" of variational inference: write the random $\mathbf z$ as a deterministic function of the parameters $\boldsymbol\nu$ and independent noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon$, so the gradient can flow through the sampling step.
