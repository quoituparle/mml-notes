3.1Norms

A norm $\|\cdot\|$ assigns every vector a length. To deserve the name it must obey three rules:

  • Absolutely homogeneous: $\|\lambda\mathbf{x}\|=|\lambda|\,\|\mathbf{x}\|$ (double the vector, double the length)
  • Triangle inequality: $\|\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|+\|\mathbf{y}\|$ (a detour is never shorter than the direct path)
  • Positive definite: $\|\mathbf{x}\|\ge0$, and $\|\mathbf{x}\|=0$ only for $\mathbf{x}=\mathbf 0$

There is more than one way to measure length. The two from the book:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|_1=\sum_{i=1}^n|x_i|\ \ \text{(Manhattan, }\ell_1\text{)},\qquad \|\mathbf{x}\|_2=\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2}=\sqrt{\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}}\ \ \text{(Euclidean, }\ell_2\text{)}$$

Manhattan length is what a taxi drives in a city grid (only along streets). Euclidean is the crow's flight. The book uses $\ell_2$ unless it says otherwise. Below, the orange curve is the set of all vectors with the same length as $\mathbf{x}$. Change $p$ and watch the shape of "equally long" change:

Side note beyond the book: the corners of the $\ell_1$ diamond sit on the axes. That is the usual intuition for why $\ell_1$ regularisation pushes some weights to exactly zero.

Going deeper: more norms, and what a norm can't do

  • The $\ell_p$ family: $\|\mathbf x\|_p=\big(\sum_i|x_i|^p\big)^{1/p}$ for $p\ge1$ is a norm ($p=1$ Manhattan, $p=2$ Euclidean). The limit $p\to\infty$ is the max norm $\|\mathbf x\|_\infty=\max_i|x_i|$. This is the shape-morphing the widget shows. For $p\lt1$ the triangle inequality fails, so it is not a norm.
  • Example: $\mathbf x=(1,-2,2)$ has $\|\mathbf x\|_1=5$, $\|\mathbf x\|_2=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$, $\|\mathbf x\|_\infty=2$. Always $\|\mathbf x\|_\infty\le\|\mathbf x\|_2\le\|\mathbf x\|_1$.
  • Normalising: for $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$ the vector $\mathbf x/\|\mathbf x\|$ has norm 1 (a unit vector), by absolute homogeneity.
  • Limits of a norm: a norm gives lengths (and distances) but no notion of angle between vectors. For angles you need an inner product, next section. Not every norm comes from an inner product ($\ell_1$ doesn't), but every inner product gives a norm.
In short. Norm = length with three sensible rules. Different norms, different shapes of "unit ball".
Check yourselfCompute $\|(3,-4)\|_1$ and $\|(3,-4)\|_2$.
Answer: $3+4=7$ and $\sqrt{9+16}=5$.

3.2Inner Products

You know the dot product: multiply component by component and add.

$$\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i=1}^n x_iy_i$$

The inner product generalises it. It is any function $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle$ that takes two vectors, returns a number, and is:

  • Bilinear: linear in each argument (it distributes over sums and pulls out scalars, like normal multiplication).
  • Symmetric: $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x}\rangle$.
  • Positive definite: $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle>0$ for every $\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf 0$ (a nonzero vector always has positive "self-overlap").

The dot product is one example; it is not the only one. The book's Example 3.3, $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=x_1y_1-(x_1y_2+x_2y_1)+2x_2y_2$, is another.

Symmetric, positive definite matrices

Here's the practical way to build inner products. Pick a basis and a matrix $A$. Then $\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=\hat{\mathbf{x}}^\top A\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ ($\hat{\mathbf x},\hat{\mathbf y}$ are the coordinates). It is a valid inner product exactly when $A$ is symmetric and positive definite:

$$A=A^\top\qquad\text{and}\qquad \mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{x}>0\ \text{ for all }\mathbf{x}\ne\mathbf 0$$

Example from the book: $A_1=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&5\end{bmatrix}$ is positive definite since $\mathbf{x}^\top A_1\mathbf{x}=(3x_1+2x_2)^2+x_2^2>0$. But $A_2=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&3\end{bmatrix}$ gives $(3x_1+2x_2)^2-x_2^2$, which is negative for $\mathbf{x}=(2,-3)$, so it fails.

Play with it: the three sliders fill in a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix. The vectors $\mathbf{x}=(2,2)$ and $\mathbf{y}=(-2,2)$ never move, but their lengths and angle do, because the ruler (the inner product) changes. The dot product says they are perpendicular. Try "diag(2,1)".

Takeaway: "orthogonal" and "length" are not absolute. They depend on which inner product you picked. Vectors orthogonal under the dot product need not be orthogonal under another.

Going deeper: the definitions, and how to test positive definiteness

A map $\Omega:V\times V\to\mathbb{R}$ is bilinear if it is linear in each argument separately: $\Omega(\lambda\mathbf x+\psi\mathbf y,\mathbf z)=\lambda\Omega(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)+\psi\Omega(\mathbf y,\mathbf z)$, and the same in the second slot. It is symmetric if $\Omega(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\Omega(\mathbf y,\mathbf x)$ and positive definite if $\Omega(\mathbf x,\mathbf x)\gt0$ for all $\mathbf x\ne\mathbf 0$. A symmetric, positive definite bilinear map is an inner product $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$, and $(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is an inner product space (a Euclidean vector space if it is the dot product).

Why every inner product is a matrix

Pick a basis $B=(\mathbf b_1,\dots,\mathbf b_n)$ and write $\mathbf x=\sum\psi_i\mathbf b_i$, $\mathbf y=\sum\lambda_j\mathbf b_j$. Bilinearity gives

$$\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=\sum_{i,j}\psi_i\langle\mathbf b_i,\mathbf b_j\rangle\lambda_j=\hat{\mathbf x}^\top A\hat{\mathbf y},\qquad A_{ij}=\langle\mathbf b_i,\mathbf b_j\rangle.$$

$A$ is symmetric (the inner product is) and positive definite. Conversely, any symmetric positive definite $A$ defines an inner product (book Thm 3.5). The dot product is $A=I$.

Book Ex. 3.3 An inner product that is not the dot product

$\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=x_1y_1-(x_1y_2+x_2y_1)+2x_2y_2=\mathbf x^\top\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{bmatrix}\mathbf y$. It is symmetric, and $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle=(x_1-x_2)^2+x_2^2\gt0$ unless $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$, so it is an inner product. (Exercise E3.1 asks you to prove it.)

Three ways to test "symmetric positive definite"

  • Complete the square: show $\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf x$ is a sum of squares that vanishes only at $\mathbf 0$.
  • Eigenvalues (Ch. 4): symmetric $A$ is positive definite iff all eigenvalues are $\gt0$.
  • $2\times2$ shortcut: $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&d\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is positive definite iff $a\gt0$ and $ad-b^2\gt0$.

Book Ex. 3.4 Positive definite or not?

$A_1=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&5\end{bmatrix}$: $\mathbf x^\top A_1\mathbf x=9x_1^2+12x_1x_2+5x_2^2=(3x_1+2x_2)^2+x_2^2\gt0$: positive definite (eigenvalues $7\pm2\sqrt{10}\gt0$, determinant $9\gt0$). $A_2=\begin{bmatrix}9&6\\6&3\end{bmatrix}$: $\mathbf x^\top A_2\mathbf x=(3x_1+2x_2)^2-x_2^2$, and at $\mathbf x=(2,-3)$ this is $36-72+27=-9\lt0$: not positive definite (determinant $-9$).

Handy consequences for SPD $A$: its kernel is $\{\mathbf 0\}$ (so it is invertible), and its diagonal entries are positive ($a_{ii}=\mathbf e_i^\top A\mathbf e_i\gt0$). Covariance matrices (Ch. 6) are the standard source of SPD matrices in ML.

In short. Inner product = bilinear + symmetric + positive definite. In coordinates it is $\mathbf{x}^\top A\mathbf{y}$ with $A$ symmetric positive definite. Dot product is the case $A=I$.
Check yourselfIs $\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\2&1\end{bmatrix}$ positive definite?
Answer: No. For $\mathbf{x}=(1,-1)$: $1-4+1=-2<0$. (The widget's "Not positive definite" preset is the same idea.)

3.3Lengths and Distances

Every inner product comes with its own norm for free:

$$\|\mathbf{x}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}\rangle}$$

(Not every norm arises this way. The Manhattan norm doesn't come from any inner product.) The two always obey the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

$$|\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle|\le\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|$$

Distance is just the length of the difference:

$$d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|=\sqrt{\langle\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y},\,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rangle}$$

A distance function (a metric) must be positive definite ($d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=0\iff\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}$), symmetric, and satisfy the triangle inequality $d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\le d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+d(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z})$.

Subtle point from the book: inner product and distance move in opposite directions. Two very similar vectors have a large inner product but a small distance.

Going deeper: why Cauchy–Schwarz holds, and lengths under other inner products

Proof of Cauchy–Schwarz. For $\mathbf y\ne\mathbf 0$ let $t=\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle/\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf y\rangle$. Then $0\le\|\mathbf x-t\mathbf y\|^2=\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle-\dfrac{\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle^2}{\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf y\rangle}$, which rearranges to $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle^2\le\|\mathbf x\|^2\|\mathbf y\|^2$. (Geometrically $t\mathbf y$ is the projection of $\mathbf x$ onto $\mathbf y$; the leftover has non-negative length.) The triangle inequality follows: $\|\mathbf x+\mathbf y\|^2=\|\mathbf x\|^2+2\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle+\|\mathbf y\|^2\le(\|\mathbf x\|+\|\mathbf y\|)^2$.

Book Ex. 3.5 Same vector, different length

Let $\mathbf x=(1,1)$. Dot product: $\|\mathbf x\|=\sqrt2$. With $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac12\\-\frac12&1\end{bmatrix}$: $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle=1-1+1=1$, so $\|\mathbf x\|=1$. Lengths depend on the inner product you chose.

Distance and metric. $d(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=\|\mathbf x-\mathbf y\|$ is a metric: $d\ge0$ with $d(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=0\iff\mathbf x=\mathbf y$ (positive definite), $d(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)=d(\mathbf y,\mathbf x)$ (symmetric), and $d(\mathbf x,\mathbf z)\le d(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)+d(\mathbf y,\mathbf z)$ (triangle inequality). A norm alone suffices to define a distance; if the norm comes from an inner product, the distance changes when you change the inner product.

In short. Length = $\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Distance = length of $x-y$. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the inner product by the product of lengths.
Check yourselfEuclidean distance between $(1,2)$ and $(4,6)$?
Answer: $\|(-3,-4)\|=5$.

3.4Angles and Orthogonality

Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees that $\frac{\langle x,y\rangle}{\|x\|\|y\|}$ lies between $-1$ and $1$, which is exactly the range of cosine. So we define the angle $\omega\in[0,\pi]$ by

$$\cos\omega=\frac{\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle}{\|\mathbf{x}\|\,\|\mathbf{y}\|}$$

Book example: $\mathbf{x}=(1,1)$, $\mathbf{y}=(1,2)$ gives $\cos\omega=3/\sqrt{10}$, so $\omega\approx18.4^\circ$. Try it, and also look at the distance (dashed line) and Cauchy–Schwarz check:

Orthogonal means the inner product is zero: $\mathbf{x}\perp\mathbf{y}\iff\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=0$. If both also have length 1, they are orthonormal. (The zero vector is orthogonal to everything. Politely awkward.)

DefOrthogonal matrix

A square matrix whose columns are orthonormal:

$$AA^\top=A^\top A=I\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A^{-1}=A^\top$$

Inverting it costs nothing: just transpose. And it moves vectors without distorting them, since $\|A\mathbf{x}\|^2=\mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^\top\mathbf{x}=\|\mathbf{x}\|^2$. Lengths and angles are preserved. These are the rotations and flips (see 3.9).

ML link. The cosine of the angle is "cosine similarity", the standard way to compare word embeddings and documents. It ignores length and only asks "do they point the same way?"

Going deeper: angles depend on the inner product; why orthogonal matrices are rigid

Book Ex. 3.7 Orthogonal under one inner product, not under another

$\mathbf x=(1,1)$, $\mathbf y=(-1,1)$. With the dot product $\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y=-1+1=0$: $\omega=90^\circ$, orthogonal. With $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]$: $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=-2+1=-1$, $\|\mathbf x\|=\|\mathbf y\|=\sqrt3$, so $\cos\omega=-\tfrac13$ and $\omega\approx109.5^\circ$: not orthogonal.

Orthogonal matrices. $A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}$ with orthonormal columns satisfies $A^\top A=AA^\top=I$, so $A^{-1}=A^\top$. Proof that it preserves length: $\|A\mathbf x\|^2=(A\mathbf x)^\top(A\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^\top A^\top A\mathbf x=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf x=\|\mathbf x\|^2$. Angles are preserved because the dot product is: $(A\mathbf x)^\top(A\mathbf y)=\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y$. These maps are exactly rotations and reflections (their determinant is $\pm1$, Ch. 4). The usual name "orthogonal" is slightly unfortunate; "orthonormal matrix" would be more accurate.

In short. Angle from $\cos\omega=\langle x,y\rangle/(\|x\|\|y\|)$. Orthogonal = inner product 0. Orthogonal matrices keep lengths and angles, with $A^{-1}=A^\top$.
Check yourselfAngle between $(1,0)$ and $(0,5)$?
Answer: dot product $0$, so $\cos\omega=0$ and $\omega=90^\circ$. (Length 5 doesn't matter.)

3.5Orthonormal Basis

In Chapter 2 a basis was just "independent vectors that span". Now we can ask for the nicest kind: basis vectors that are orthogonal to each other and have length 1.

$$\langle\mathbf{b}_i,\mathbf{b}_j\rangle=0\ (i\ne j),\qquad\langle\mathbf{b}_i,\mathbf{b}_i\rangle=1$$

That is an orthonormal basis (ONB). (Orthogonal only, without the length-1 condition, is an orthogonal basis.) The standard basis is one. Another in $\mathbb{R}^2$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$.

The payoff: with an ONB, finding coordinates needs no equation solving. Each coordinate is a single dot product, $\lambda_i=\mathbf{b}_i^\top\mathbf{x}$.

Rotate the basis with θ. The coordinates change but the recipe never does: just dot with each basis vector. Gram–Schmidt (3.8) turns any basis into an ONB.

Going deeper: why ONBs are so convenient

In an ONB $(\mathbf b_1,\dots,\mathbf b_n)$, taking the inner product of $\mathbf x=\sum\lambda_i\mathbf b_i$ with $\mathbf b_j$ kills every term except $\lambda_j$ (orthogonality) and leaves $\lambda_j\langle\mathbf b_j,\mathbf b_j\rangle=\lambda_j$ (unit length), hence $\lambda_j=\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf b_j\rangle$. Pythagoras comes for free: $\|\mathbf x\|^2=\sum_i\lambda_i^2$. Stacking the basis vectors as columns of $B$ gives an orthogonal matrix, with coordinates $\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top\mathbf x$ and reconstruction $\mathbf x=B\boldsymbol\lambda$.

Book Ex. 3.8 A non-standard ONB

$\mathbf b_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$, $\mathbf b_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$: $\mathbf b_1^\top\mathbf b_2=\tfrac12(1-1)=0$ and each has length $\sqrt{\tfrac12+\tfrac12}=1$. So it is an ONB of $\mathbb{R}^2$. For $\mathbf x=(3,1)$: $\lambda_1=\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2}$, $\lambda_2=\tfrac{2}{\sqrt2}$ and $\lambda_1^2+\lambda_2^2=8+2=10=\|\mathbf x\|^2$ ✓.

Making one: the Gram–Schmidt process (section 3.8) turns any basis into an orthogonal/orthonormal one. For an orthogonal-but-not-normalised basis, divide each vector by its length.

In short. ONB = orthogonal + unit length. Coordinates are $\lambda_i=\mathbf{b}_i^\top\mathbf{x}$.
Check yourselfIs $\{(1,0),(1,1)\}$ an ONB?
Answer: No. Their dot product is $1\ne0$ (and $(1,1)$ has length $\sqrt2$).

3.6Orthogonal Complement

Take a subspace $U$ of a $D$-dimensional space $V$ with $\dim U=M$. Its orthogonal complement $U^\perp$ is the set of all vectors orthogonal to every vector of $U$. It is itself a subspace, of dimension $D-M$, and $U\cap U^\perp=\{\mathbf 0\}$. Together they cover everything uniquely:

$$\mathbf{x}=\sum_{m=1}^M\lambda_m\mathbf{b}_m+\sum_{j=1}^{D-M}\psi_j\mathbf{b}^\perp_j$$

(with $\mathbf{b}_m$ a basis of $U$ and $\mathbf{b}^\perp_j$ a basis of $U^\perp$). Every vector splits into "the part in $U$" plus "the part perpendicular to $U$":

In 3D: a plane $U$ through the origin has a 1-dimensional complement, the line along its normal vector $\mathbf{w}$. That is why a plane can be described by one vector: "all vectors orthogonal to $\mathbf{w}$".

Going deeper: normal vectors and hyperplanes

If $U$ is a hyperplane (dimension $D-1$) in $\mathbb{R}^D$, then $U^\perp$ is a line spanned by a unit normal vector $\mathbf w$, and $U=\{\mathbf x:\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x=0\}$. Example: $U=\{x_1+x_2+x_3=0\}\subset\mathbb{R}^3$ has $\mathbf w=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$, $\dim U^\perp=3-2=1$. Any $\mathbf x$ splits as $\mathbf x=(\mathbf x-\lambda\mathbf w)+\lambda\mathbf w$ with $\lambda=\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x$: the second part is the component perpendicular to the plane.

A fact worth remembering (not in the book's text but standard, and it ties back to Chapter 2): for a matrix $A$, the kernel of $A$ is the orthogonal complement of its row space, because $A\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ says $\mathbf x$ is perpendicular to every row. Dimensions agree: $(n-\operatorname{rk}A)+\operatorname{rk}A=n$.

Hyperplanes also describe decision boundaries: a linear classifier (Ch. 12) splits space by the sign of $\mathbf w^\top\mathbf x+b$.

In short. $U^\perp$ = everything perpendicular to $U$; dimensions add up to the whole space; every vector splits uniquely into a $U$-part and a $U^\perp$-part.
Check yourself$U$ is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$. What is $\dim U^\perp$?
Answer: $3-1=2$. It is the plane perpendicular to the line.

3.7Inner Product of Functions

A vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$ is really a function with $n$ values. Let the number of values go to infinity and the sum in the dot product becomes an integral:

$$\langle u,v\rangle=\int_a^b u(x)\,v(x)\,dx$$

All the geometry still works. If the integral is $0$, the functions are orthogonal. The area under $u\cdot v$ is the inner product, so orthogonal means "positive and negative areas cancel":

Book Example 3.9: $\sin$ and $\cos$ are orthogonal on $[-\pi,\pi]$, because $\sin x\cos x$ is an odd function. More generally $\{1,\cos x,\cos 2x,\dots\}$ are mutually orthogonal there. Projecting a function onto such a family is the idea of Fourier series. (The book notes that making this fully rigorous needs more analysis than it covers.)

Going deeper: lengths of functions, and a warning

Everything from the dot product carries over: $\|u\|=\sqrt{\int_a^bu(x)^2dx}$ is the length of a function and $\|u-v\|$ the distance between two. Example on $[-\pi,\pi]$: $\|\sin\|^2=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\sin^2x\,dx=\pi$, so $\|\sin\|=\sqrt\pi$, while $\langle\sin,\cos\rangle=0$ (odd integrand). The set $\{1,\cos x,\cos2x,\dots\}$ is mutually orthogonal there; projecting a function onto a family like this is exactly what a Fourier series does.

Warning: unlike finite-dimensional vectors, such integrals can be infinite, and a rigorous treatment needs measure theory (Hilbert spaces). The book stays informal here, and so will we.

Same idea for random variables: $\mathbb{E}[XY]$ behaves like an inner product (Ch. 6.4.6), so covariance is an inner product and correlation is a cosine.

In short. Functions are vectors; sum → integral; orthogonal ⇔ the integral of the product is 0.
Check yourselfWhy is $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}x\cos x\,dx=0$ without computing?
Answer: $x$ is odd and $\cos x$ is even, so the product is odd, and an odd function integrates to 0 over a symmetric interval.

3.8Orthogonal Projections

High-dimensional data is hard to look at and often mostly redundant. A projection squeezes it into a lower-dimensional subspace while keeping as much as possible. Formally, a linear map $\pi:V\to U$ is a projection if applying it twice changes nothing:

$$\pi^2=\pi\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad P_\pi^2=P_\pi$$

(Once you've been squashed onto the line, squashing again does nothing.) We use the orthogonal projection: the point of $U$ closest to $\mathbf{x}$.

Onto a line

Let $U$ be the line spanned by $\mathbf{b}$. The closest point is $\pi_U(\mathbf{x})=\lambda\mathbf{b}$ for some $\lambda$, and the error $\mathbf{x}-\lambda\mathbf{b}$ must be orthogonal to $\mathbf{b}$. Writing that out:

$$\langle\mathbf{x}-\lambda\mathbf{b},\mathbf{b}\rangle=0\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda=\frac{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{b}},\qquad \pi_U(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2}\mathbf{b},\qquad P_\pi=\frac{\mathbf{b}\mathbf{b}^\top}{\|\mathbf{b}\|^2}$$

Book Example 3.10: for $\mathbf{b}=(1,2,2)$ and $\mathbf{x}=(1,1,1)$, $\lambda=5/9$ and $\pi_U(\mathbf{x})=\frac19(5,10,10)$. Note $P_\pi$ is symmetric, rank 1, and $\|\pi_U(\mathbf x)\|=|\cos\omega|\,\|\mathbf x\|$, the trigonometry you remember.

Why ML cares: the best line through data

Project every data point onto a line and add up the squared errors (the book calls this reconstruction error). The best line is the one that keeps the most information, i.e. has the smallest error. This is the heart of PCA (Ch. 10):

Onto a general subspace

Now let $U$ be spanned by several basis vectors, collected as columns of $B=[\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_m]$. Same idea: $\pi_U(\mathbf{x})=B\boldsymbol{\lambda}$, and the error must be orthogonal to every $\mathbf{b}_i$, i.e. $B^\top(\mathbf{x}-B\boldsymbol\lambda)=\mathbf 0$. Rearranged, this is the normal equation:

$$B^\top B\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top\mathbf{x}\ \Rightarrow\ \boldsymbol\lambda=(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top\mathbf{x},\qquad \pi_U(\mathbf{x})=B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top\mathbf{x}$$

So $P_\pi=B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top$. For one vector this collapses to the line formula above. The matrix $(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top$ is the pseudo-inverse from Chapter 2. Here is the book's Example 3.11 in 3D (the plane is $U$, drag the view to see the error is perpendicular):

ShortcutIf the basis is orthonormal

Then $B^\top B=I$ and everything simplifies: $\pi_U(\mathbf{x})=BB^\top\mathbf{x}$ with coordinates $\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top\mathbf{x}$. No inverse needed. That is why we like ONBs.

ML link (least squares). If $A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}$ has no solution, $\mathbf b$ is outside the column space of $A$. The best you can do is project $\mathbf{b}$ onto that column space: the closest reachable point. That is the least-squares solution, the engine of linear regression (Ch. 9). The book also notes in practice a small "jitter" $\epsilon I$ is often added to $B^\top B$ for numerical stability.

Gram–Schmidt: manufacturing an orthogonal basis

Given any basis $(\mathbf{b}_1,\dots,\mathbf{b}_n)$, build an orthogonal one using projections. Keep the first vector, then for each next vector subtract its projection onto what you already built:

$$\mathbf{u}_1=\mathbf{b}_1,\qquad \mathbf{u}_k=\mathbf{b}_k-\pi_{\operatorname{span}[\mathbf{u}_1,\dots,\mathbf{u}_{k-1}]}(\mathbf{b}_k)$$

Normalise each $\mathbf{u}_k$ and you have an ONB spanning the same space.

Onto an affine subspace

For a shifted subspace $L=\mathbf{x}_0+U$: subtract the support point, project onto $U$, add it back:

$$\pi_L(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}_0+\pi_U(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0),\qquad d(\mathbf{x},L)=d(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0,\,\pi_U(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_0))$$

Going deeper: worked projections, the projection error, and the details behind the formulas

Why the normal equation is "closest point"

The point $\pi_U(\mathbf x)\in U$ closest to $\mathbf x$ must make the error $\mathbf x-\pi_U(\mathbf x)$ orthogonal to all of $U$ (otherwise a small move inside $U$ would shrink it). Orthogonality to each basis vector $\mathbf b_i$ is $B^\top(\mathbf x-B\boldsymbol\lambda)=\mathbf 0$, the normal equation $B^\top B\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top\mathbf x$. $B^\top B$ is invertible because the $\mathbf b_i$ are independent. If your spanning set is not a basis, find a basis first (Chapter 2). The distance from $\mathbf x$ to $U$ is the projection (reconstruction) error $\|\mathbf x-\pi_U(\mathbf x)\|$.

Also true: $P_\pi^2=P_\pi$ (project twice = project once) and $P_\pi$ is symmetric. With a general inner product replace $B^\top$ by $B^\top A$ throughout (see E3.6).

Book Ex. 3.10 Projection onto a line in $\mathbb{R}^3$

$\mathbf b=(1,2,2)$, $\mathbf b^\top\mathbf b=9$, so $P_\pi=\tfrac19\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\2&4&4\\2&4&4\end{bmatrix}$. For $\mathbf x=(1,1,1)$: $\pi_U(\mathbf x)=\tfrac19(5,10,10)=\tfrac59\mathbf b$, so $\lambda=\tfrac59$. It lies in $\operatorname{span}[\mathbf b]$, and projecting again changes nothing ($P_\pi\pi_U(\mathbf x)=\pi_U(\mathbf x)$).

Book Ex. 3.11 Projection onto a plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$

$U=\operatorname{span}\big[(1,1,1),(0,1,2)\big]$, $\mathbf x=(6,0,0)$. $B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\1&1\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, $B^\top B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}3&3\\3&5\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, $B^\top\mathbf x=(6,0)$. Solving $B^\top B\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top\mathbf x$: $\boldsymbol\lambda=(5,-3)$. Then

$$\pi_U(\mathbf x)=B\boldsymbol\lambda=(5,2,-1),\qquad \|\mathbf x-\pi_U(\mathbf x)\|=\|(1,-2,1)\|=\sqrt6,\qquad P_\pi=\tfrac16\begin{bmatrix}5&2&-1\\2&2&2\\-1&2&5\end{bmatrix}.$$

Checks: $(1,-2,1)\cdot(1,1,1)=0$ and $(1,-2,1)\cdot(0,1,2)=0$ ✓ (error is orthogonal to $U$).

Gram–Schmidt in detail

From a basis $(\mathbf b_1,\dots,\mathbf b_n)$ build orthogonal $(\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_n)$ with the same spans: $\mathbf u_1=\mathbf b_1$, $\mathbf u_k=\mathbf b_k-\pi_{\operatorname{span}[\mathbf u_1,\dots,\mathbf u_{k-1}]}(\mathbf b_k)$. Because the $\mathbf u_i$ are already orthogonal, that projection is just the sum of the 1-D projections $\sum_{i\lt k}\frac{\mathbf u_i^\top\mathbf b_k}{\mathbf u_i^\top\mathbf u_i}\mathbf u_i$. Normalise at the end for an ONB.

Book Ex. 3.12 Gram–Schmidt in $\mathbb{R}^2$

$\mathbf b_1=(2,0)$, $\mathbf b_2=(1,1)$. $\mathbf u_1=(2,0)$; $\pi_{\operatorname{span}[\mathbf u_1]}(\mathbf b_2)=\frac{2\cdot1}{4}(2,0)=(1,0)$; $\mathbf u_2=\mathbf b_2-(1,0)=(0,1)$. Orthogonal ✓ ($\mathbf u_1^\top\mathbf u_2=0$).

Numerical note (from the book)

In practice one often adds a small "jitter" $\epsilon I$ to $B^\top B$ for numerical stability, and avoids explicit inverses. This "ridge" idea returns in Chapter 9.

Projections onto affine subspaces

$L=\mathbf x_0+U$: shift by $-\mathbf x_0$, project onto $U$, shift back: $\pi_L(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x_0+\pi_U(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0)$ and $d(\mathbf x,L)=d(\mathbf x-\mathbf x_0,U)$. This is how the distance from a point to a hyperplane (the SVM margin, Ch. 12) is computed.

In short. Projection = closest point in the subspace; error ⟂ subspace. Line: $P=bb^\top/\|b\|^2$. General: $P=B(B^\top B)^{-1}B^\top$. ONB: $P=BB^\top$. Gram–Schmidt = subtract projections.
Check yourselfProject $(3,4)$ onto the $x_1$-axis. What are $\pi$, $P_\pi$ and the error?
Answer: $b=(1,0)$: $\pi=(3,0)$, $P=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, error $\|(0,4)\|=4$.

3.9Rotations

A rotation turns the plane by an angle $\theta$ about the origin (positive = counterclockwise). It is a linear map, so by Chapter 2 its matrix has columns = where the standard basis lands: $\mathbf{e}_1\mapsto(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $\mathbf{e}_2\mapsto(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$. Hence

$$R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}$$

This is an orthogonal matrix, so it preserves lengths and angles. The "flip" slider adds a mirror; the result is still orthogonal but has determinant $-1$ (book: "rotations, with the possibility of flips").

Higher dimensions

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ you rotate around an axis. Fix one coordinate, rotate the other two. For example about $\mathbf{e}_3$ (looking at the axis head-on from its tip):

$$R_3(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}$$

In $n$ dimensions, a Givens rotation $R_{ij}(\theta)$ is the identity with just four entries changed: $r_{ii}=\cos\theta$, $r_{ij}=-\sin\theta$, $r_{ji}=\sin\theta$, $r_{jj}=\cos\theta$. It rotates in the $(i,j)$ plane and leaves all other coordinates alone.

Properties

  • Rotations preserve distances: $\|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|=\|R\mathbf{x}-R\mathbf{y}\|$.
  • Rotations preserve angles.
  • In 2D, rotations commute: $R(\phi)R(\theta)=R(\theta)R(\phi)$. In 3D and higher they generally do not: order matters (try turning a book 90° about two different axes in both orders).

Going deeper: rotations in 3D and beyond, with a non-commuting example

Rotating the basis by $\theta$ in the plane gives the columns $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$ and $(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$: $R(\theta)=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Book Figure 3.14 uses $\theta=112.5^\circ$, giving $R\approx\left[\begin{smallmatrix}-0.38&-0.92\\0.92&-0.38\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Positive angles turn counterclockwise.

In $\mathbb{R}^3$ there is one rotation matrix per axis (the coordinate along the axis stays fixed; "counterclockwise" is as seen looking from the axis tip toward the origin):

$$R_1(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\0&\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\ R_2(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&0&\sin\theta\\0&1&0\\-\sin\theta&0&\cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\ R_3(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta&0\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

In $n$ dimensions, rotate in one plane and fix the rest. The Givens rotation $R_{ij}(\theta)$ equals $I_n$ except $r_{ii}=\cos\theta$, $r_{ij}=-\sin\theta$, $r_{ji}=\sin\theta$, $r_{jj}=\cos\theta$.

3D rotations do not commute

Take $\mathbf x=\mathbf e_2$ and angle $90^\circ$. Rotate about $\mathbf e_1$ first, then about $\mathbf e_3$: $R_1(90^\circ)\mathbf e_2=\mathbf e_3$, then $R_3(90^\circ)\mathbf e_3=\mathbf e_3$. Reverse the order: $R_3(90^\circ)\mathbf e_2=-\mathbf e_1$, then $R_1(90^\circ)(-\mathbf e_1)=-\mathbf e_1$. Results $\mathbf e_3$ versus $-\mathbf e_1$: order matters (in 2D it doesn't: $R(\phi)R(\theta)=R(\theta)R(\phi)$).

Properties (all because $R$ is orthogonal): distances and angles are preserved, $R^{-1}=R^\top=R(-\theta)$ in 2D, and rotations about the same point in 2D form an Abelian group. Uses: computer graphics, robot joints, and "augmenting" image data by rotating it.

In short. $R(\theta)$ = columns are the rotated basis vectors. Orthogonal, so $R^{-1}=R^\top$ and nothing gets distorted.
Check yourselfWhere does $R(90^\circ)$ send $(1,0)$?
Answer: First column of $R(90^\circ)$ is $(\cos90^\circ,\sin90^\circ)=(0,1)$.

Exercises with solutions

All ten exercises from the book's Chapter 3 (numbers refer to the book). Try them first, then open the solutions. Every numerical answer was checked by computer.

E3.1 · Show a function is an inner product book 3.1

Show that $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle:=x_1y_1-(x_1y_2+x_2y_1)+2x_2y_2$ is an inner product on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Solution

Bilinear: each term is (a coordinate of $\mathbf x$)$\times$(a coordinate of $\mathbf y$) times a constant, so the map is linear in $\mathbf x$ for fixed $\mathbf y$ and vice versa. Symmetric: swapping $\mathbf x\leftrightarrow\mathbf y$ gives $y_1x_1-(y_1x_2+y_2x_1)+2y_2x_2$, the same expression. Positive definite: $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle=x_1^2-2x_1x_2+2x_2^2=(x_1-x_2)^2+x_2^2\ge0$, and it is $0$ only if $x_2=0$ and $x_1=x_2$, i.e. $\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$. ✓ (Equivalently $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is symmetric with $a_{11}=1\gt0$, $\det=1\gt0$.)

E3.2 · Is it an inner product? book 3.2

On $\mathbb{R}^2$ let $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle:=\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf y$ with $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. Is this an inner product?

Solution

No. $A$ is not symmetric, so the map is not symmetric: $\langle\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\rangle=A_{12}=0$ but $\langle\mathbf e_2,\mathbf e_1\rangle=A_{21}=1$. (Positive definiteness of the quadratic form alone is not enough; symmetry is a separate requirement.)

E3.3 · Distances with two inner products book 3.3

Compute $d(\mathbf x,\mathbf y)$ for $\mathbf x=(1,2,3)$, $\mathbf y=(-1,-1,0)$ using (a) the dot product (b) $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf y$, $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\1&3&-1\\0&-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

Solution

$\mathbf v=\mathbf x-\mathbf y=(2,3,3)$. (a) $d=\sqrt{4+9+9}=\sqrt{22}\approx4.69$. (b) $A\mathbf v=(2\cdot2+3,\ 2+9-3,\ -3+6)=(7,8,3)$ and $\mathbf v^\top A\mathbf v=14+24+9=47$, so $d=\sqrt{47}\approx6.86$. Same two points, different distance.

E3.4 · Angles with two inner products book 3.4

Angle between $\mathbf x=(1,2)$ and $\mathbf y=(-1,-1)$ using (a) the dot product (b) $\mathbf x^\top B\mathbf y$ with $B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{smallmatrix}\right]$.

Solution

(a) $\mathbf x^\top\mathbf y=-3$, $\|\mathbf x\|=\sqrt5$, $\|\mathbf y\|=\sqrt2$: $\cos\omega=-3/\sqrt{10}\approx-0.949$, so $\omega\approx2.82$ rad $\approx161.6^\circ$.

(b) $B\mathbf y=(-3,-4)$, so $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=-3-8=-11$. $B\mathbf x=(4,7)$, so $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf x\rangle=4+14=18$. $\langle\mathbf y,\mathbf y\rangle=(-1)(-3)+(-1)(-4)=7$. $\cos\omega=-11/\sqrt{18\cdot7}=-11/\sqrt{126}\approx-0.980$, so $\omega\approx2.94$ rad $\approx168.5^\circ$.

E3.5 · Projection onto a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^5$ book 3.5

Let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^5$ be

$$U=\operatorname{span}\big[(0,-1,2,0,2),\ (1,-3,1,-1,2),\ (-3,4,1,2,1),\ (-1,-3,5,0,7)\big]$$

and $\mathbf x=(-1,-9,-1,4,1)$. (a) Find $\pi_U(\mathbf x)$. (b) Find $d(\mathbf x,U)$.

Solution

Step 1: a basis. Row-reducing the four vectors shows the rank is 3; the 4th vector is $1\cdot\mathbf v_1+2\cdot\mathbf v_2+1\cdot\mathbf v_3$. So $\mathbf v_1,\mathbf v_2,\mathbf v_3$ is a basis; let $B=[\mathbf v_1\ \mathbf v_2\ \mathbf v_3]$.

Step 2: normal equation. $B^\top B=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}9&9&0\\9&16&-14\\0&-14&31\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, $B^\top\mathbf x=(9,23,-25)$. Solving gives $\boldsymbol\lambda=(-3,4,1)$ (check row 2: $-27+64-14=23$ ✓).

(a) $\pi_U(\mathbf x)=B\boldsymbol\lambda=(1,-5,-1,-2,3)$.

(b) $\mathbf x-\pi_U(\mathbf x)=(-2,-4,0,6,-2)$, so $d(\mathbf x,U)=\sqrt{4+16+0+36+4}=\sqrt{60}=2\sqrt{15}\approx7.75$. (It is orthogonal to each $\mathbf v_i$: e.g. $(-2,-4,0,6,-2)\cdot\mathbf v_1=4+0+0-4=0$ ✓.)

E3.6 · Projection with a non-standard inner product book 3.6

On $\mathbb{R}^3$ use $\langle\mathbf x,\mathbf y\rangle=\mathbf x^\top A\mathbf y$ with $A=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$. (a) Project $\mathbf e_2$ onto $U=\operatorname{span}[\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_3]$. (b) Compute $d(\mathbf e_2,U)$.

Solution

Orthogonality now means $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle=0$, so the normal equation is $(B^\top AB)\boldsymbol\lambda=B^\top A\mathbf x$ with $B=[\mathbf e_1\ \mathbf e_3]$.

$B^\top AB=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}A_{11}&A_{13}\\A_{31}&A_{33}\end{smallmatrix}\right]=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ and $B^\top A\mathbf e_2=(A_{12},A_{32})=(1,-1)$. So $\boldsymbol\lambda=(\tfrac12,-\tfrac12)$.

(a) $\pi_U(\mathbf e_2)=\tfrac12\mathbf e_1-\tfrac12\mathbf e_3=(\tfrac12,0,-\tfrac12)$.

(b) Error $\mathbf v=\mathbf e_2-\pi_U(\mathbf e_2)=(-\tfrac12,1,\tfrac12)$, $A\mathbf v=(0,1,0)$, so $\|\mathbf v\|_A^2=\mathbf v^\top A\mathbf v=1$ and $d(\mathbf e_2,U)=1$. Check: $B^\top A\mathbf v=(0,0)$, so the error is $A$-orthogonal to $U$ ✓. (Under the ordinary dot product the answer would be different, since "closest" depends on the inner product.)

(c) Sketch: the plane $U$ is the $x_1x_3$-plane; $\mathbf e_2$ sticks out of it, and its projection is the point $(\tfrac12,0,-\tfrac12)$ in the plane.

E3.7 · Projections and their complements book 3.7

Let $\pi$ be an endomorphism of $V$. (a) Show $\pi$ is a projection iff $\mathrm{id}_V-\pi$ is. (b) If $\pi$ is a projection, find $\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{id}-\pi)$ and $\ker(\mathrm{id}-\pi)$ in terms of $\operatorname{Im}\pi$, $\ker\pi$.

Solution

(a) If $\pi^2=\pi$ then $(\mathrm{id}-\pi)^2=\mathrm{id}-2\pi+\pi^2=\mathrm{id}-\pi$. Conversely, if $\sigma=\mathrm{id}-\pi$ satisfies $\sigma^2=\sigma$, apply the same computation to $\sigma$: $\pi=\mathrm{id}-\sigma$ is a projection. ✓

(b) $\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{id}-\pi)=\ker\pi$ and $\ker(\mathrm{id}-\pi)=\operatorname{Im}\pi$. Proof: if $\pi\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$ then $(\mathrm{id}-\pi)\mathbf x=\mathbf x$, so $\mathbf x\in\operatorname{Im}(\mathrm{id}-\pi)$; conversely $\pi(\mathbf x-\pi\mathbf x)=\pi\mathbf x-\pi^2\mathbf x=\mathbf 0$. Next, $(\mathrm{id}-\pi)\mathbf x=\mathbf 0\iff\mathbf x=\pi\mathbf x$; this forces $\mathbf x\in\operatorname{Im}\pi$, and if $\mathbf x=\pi\mathbf y$ then $\pi\mathbf x=\pi^2\mathbf y=\pi\mathbf y=\mathbf x$. ✓ (Intuition: $\mathrm{id}-\pi$ keeps the part that $\pi$ throws away.)

E3.8 · Gram–Schmidt in $\mathbb{R}^3$ book 3.8

Turn the basis $\mathbf b_1=(1,1,1)$, $\mathbf b_2=(-1,2,0)$ of a plane $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ into an ONB.

Solution

$\mathbf u_1=\mathbf b_1=(1,1,1)$. $\mathbf u_1^\top\mathbf b_2=1$, $\mathbf u_1^\top\mathbf u_1=3$, so $\pi(\mathbf b_2)=\tfrac13(1,1,1)$ and $\mathbf u_2=\mathbf b_2-\tfrac13\mathbf u_1=(-\tfrac43,\tfrac53,-\tfrac13)=\tfrac13(-4,5,-1)$. Check $\mathbf u_1^\top\mathbf u_2=\tfrac13(-4+5-1)=0$ ✓. Normalising ($\|\mathbf u_1\|=\sqrt3$, $\|(-4,5,-1)\|=\sqrt{42}$):

$$\mathbf c_1=\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1),\qquad\mathbf c_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt{42}}(-4,5,-1).$$

E3.9 · Inequalities via Cauchy–Schwarz book 3.9

Let $x_1,\dots,x_n\gt0$ with $x_1+\dots+x_n=1$. Show (a) $\sum x_i^2\ge\frac1n$ (b) $\sum\frac1{x_i}\ge n^2$.

Solution

Use $(\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b)^2\le\|\mathbf a\|^2\|\mathbf b\|^2$ with the dot product.

(a) $\mathbf a=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, $\mathbf b=(1,\dots,1)$: $\big(\sum x_i\big)^2\le\big(\sum x_i^2\big)\cdot n$, i.e. $1\le n\sum x_i^2$. ✓

(b) $\mathbf a=(\sqrt{x_1},\dots,\sqrt{x_n})$, $\mathbf b=(1/\sqrt{x_1},\dots,1/\sqrt{x_n})$: $\mathbf a^\top\mathbf b=n$, so $n^2\le\big(\sum x_i\big)\big(\sum\frac1{x_i}\big)=\sum\frac1{x_i}$. ✓ (Equality in both when all $x_i=\tfrac1n$.)

E3.10 · Rotate vectors book 3.10

Rotate $\mathbf x_1=(2,3)$ and $\mathbf x_2=(0,-1)$ by $30^\circ$.

Solution

$R(30^\circ)=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\sqrt3}{2}&-\frac12\\\frac12&\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\end{bmatrix}$. Then

$$R\mathbf x_1=\Big(\sqrt3-\tfrac32,\ 1+\tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}\Big)\approx(0.232,\ 3.598),\qquad R\mathbf x_2=\Big(\tfrac12,\ -\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\Big)\approx(0.5,\ -0.866).$$

Sanity check: lengths are preserved ($\|\mathbf x_1\|=\sqrt{13}$ before and after). Try these in the rotation widget above.
